## Most important corrections

- **Flying Dutchman:** for the 11 July 1881 sighting the two princes were aboard HMS Inconstant, not HMS Bacchante. The printed entry gives no position, so "Bass Strait" was removed. Farm Cove arrival was 14 July; the princes landed on 15 July. Heine's Schnabelewopski is 1834. Several plot details of the 1821 Blackwood's story were fixed (no first name for Vanderdecken, the wording of the oath, who weights the letters).
- **Baba Yaga:** map point corrected to Boguchar at 49.9347, 40.5544 (GeoNames). The hut-turning phrase was confirmed in Afanasyev's tale no. 102 "Baba-Yaga" and is now documented.
- **Jersey Devil:** the Mary vs Deborah and 1736 will claims, the two Pinelands PDFs, and the Daniel/Titan Leeds and Franklin dates were corrected.
- **Banshee:** Schools' Collection years are 1937–1939. The Cú Chulainn washer belongs to a late retelling only. Craglea has no cave in the record. The Clontarf tale has no exact date. The Dysert O'Dea 1318 apparition is not Aibhill. Lady Fanshawe's memoir names no house; Bunratty as the map point is a scholar's guess and is labelled that way.
- **Yuki-onna:** Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyo is 1776, not 1779. Hearn's Chofu note was confirmed in the Gutenberg text of his introduction (dated 20 January 1904), together with his remark that he did not know whether the tale had ever been written in Japanese; that remark is now a documented fact (f-30).
- **Vetala:** in Tawney's last chapter it is the king, not the spirit, who asks that the twenty-five tales be famous. The pet monkey that bursts the fruit, the ten years of daily fruit and the heap of jewels in the treasury were confirmed in chapter 75 and restored after the agent had removed them. Tawney names an asoka tree early and a simsapa later; Burton has a mimosa.
- **Spring-heeled Jack:** later books add named victims and deaths with no contemporary record; all removed. The Clapham Common tale of October 1837 is told-side only. The "Newport" lead is the Newport Arch in Lincoln (1877), not a 1904 report; the 1904 report is Everton, Liverpool. The Marquess of Waterford claim is told-side only and can never be the true version in a run.
- **Chupacabra:** the reviewer re-read four sources the agent had only skimmed and confirmed them. Skeptoid gives the Canóvanas creature as about three feet tall and calls larger heights a misreport; Colavito traces the name to the nightjar (chotacabra). The Canóvanas witness stays unnamed as a private person. The laboratory-origin version can never be the true version.
- **Draugr:** the sagadb.org English texts were identified as the public-domain Magnússon and Morris translations (Grettis saga 1869, Eyrbyggja saga 1892). Key saga facts were re-read and confirmed. Endings are told in one neutral clause, and the Fróðá haunting ends through the door-court, a legal hearing, with no religious rite.
- **El Naddaha:** the thinnest record so far. No folklore study naming the figure was read; Blackman's 1927 book was checked in part and records related beliefs (a double that calls a sleeper by name, a canal spot women avoided) but not the Naddaha by name. The account says so openly. The 1975 film details were confirmed on Arabic Wikipedia (still `[R]`). The "bride of the Nile" claim is left out as a separate, disputed story.
- **El Coco:** strongest sourcing so far: Covarrubias (1611), the Diccionario de Autoridades (1729), Barros (1563), Bluteau (1712) and Rodríguez Marín (1882) were read in scans. The popular lullaby line "te comerá" (will eat you) is not in the 1882 text, which says "carries off", so the "eats" wording is told. Covarrubias's racially loaded etymology was removed by the reviewer.
- **Gumiho:** the bead tale, its two types and its local attachments were read in the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (Academy of Korean Studies); the Shanhaijing Qingqiu passages and the Samguk sagi entry for 659 were read on Wikisource. "A hundred scholars" was corrected to "a hundred students" (the entry says school pupils). Guo Pu's peace-omen reading and a Goguryeo white-fox omen were removed as unconfirmed.

## How unverified claims are handled

- Folklore claims that could not be traced (for example the zoo reward, Decatur's cannonball, the Jeffries confession, the washing beetle, Fokke's voyage length) moved to the "told" side.
- Standard facts of scholarship that are only confirmed through reference works (for example Lomonosov's 1755 grammar, Afanasyev's nearly 600 tales, Rovinsky's reading) are tagged `[R]`. They are not folklore, so they are not "told"; they can fill date, name and object puzzles but never a true-or-told puzzle. The rule is in [legend-format.md](../../content/legend-format.md).

## Still open before launch

- Flying Dutchman: Thomas Moore 1806, the Barrington forgery claim, Heine's and Wagner's plot details, the Britannica 1911 Falkenberg entry.
- Baba Yaga: "seven tales" rests on reference works only.
- Jersey Devil: January 1909 newspaper pages, the Leeds will, the 1859 Atlantic scan and the 1939 WPA guide scan were not opened directly.
- Banshee: Lysaght's book itself, two comb-story entries on dúchas.ie, a few Wikipedia-only details.
- Yuki-onna: the 1685 Sogi collection page, Sekien's page and the five regional tellings rest on reference works (`[R]`).
- Vetala: Burton's text was read only in part; the 1805 Hindustani version, the recensions and the translation and edition year spans are `[R]`.
- Spring-heeled Jack: the 1838 newspaper pages and Mike Dash's 1996 paper were not read directly; press facts rest on two scholarly articles (Mackley 2016, Bell) that quote them. The day of the Mansion House session (8 or 9 January) and the first print of the full name are unsettled.
- Chupacabra: no 1995 or 1996 newspaper or radio record was reached; the March 1995 sheep, the 150-animal count, the mayor's hunt, Puebla and Miami 1996 are `[R]`.
- Draugr: Chadwick (1946) and Jakobsson (2011) were not read in full; the word meanings and the dating of Eyrbyggja saga are `[R]`; the Laxdæla translator on sagadb.org is not confirmed.
- El Naddaha: El-Shamy's Folktales of Egypt (1980) and the unread part of Blackman may mention the figure; the earliest dated written mention and the form and collection of Idris's work are unconfirmed. An Arabic-language folklore source would strengthen this legend most.
- El Naddaha, Arabic sources pass: Shawqi Abdel Hakim's folklore study (Hindawi edition) names al-Naddahat among spirit beliefs that still circulate, so the line saying she lives only in speech and fiction was corrected. Ahmad Amin's 1953 dictionary has no Naddaha entry in the OCR text read. Two newspaper pieces add the old-woman form and the use of the story to keep children away. A field-collected Arabic record with dates and places is still missing.
- Gumiho: the National Folk Museum encyclopedia, Chinese Text Project, and three scholarly articles could not be opened; the Chinese, Japanese and modern-history chapters rest on reference works and are tagged `[R]`; the set number of days a fox must keep a rule to become human has no source and stays on the told side.
- El Coco: the newspaper of Goya's 6 February 1799 notice (Diario de Madrid or Gaceta de Madrid) is named differently in the sources; the RAE and Prado pages could not be opened; the earliest lullaby text read is from 1882.

None of the open items is used as a documented answer in a true-or-told puzzle.
