## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] The paper door glows from the lantern in the courtyard, and on it a woman's shadow lies still. A young man, awake when he should be asleep over his books, watches it. The shadow has a long skirt and a neat, bowed head. Behind it, low and faint, something moves that no skirt makes: a slow fan of shapes, as if the lantern light were counting. He counts them, and loses count at nine. Outside, the stream under the footbridge keeps talking to itself, and the reeds at the wall shiver with no wind. In an old tale he once heard, a bead passes from one mouth to another. He tries to remember which way he is meant to look.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · A Fox That Outlived Its Years
anchor: gumiho--ch-1
sources: S5, S6
In Korea a fox is first an animal of hillsides and field edges. The stories begin when one of them lives too long. The years pile up, the tails multiply to nine, and the fox learns to walk upright in the shape of a woman. The Korean name for her is gumiho, written with the characters for nine, tail and fox. In modern Korean the word has widened to cover every fox spirit in the older sources. Reference works add that it once named a magical fox in Joseon-era novels, and that it was also used as an insult for treacherous officials (S6). The word is younger and looser than the figure it now seems to name.

The oldest Korean writing about foxes is quieter than the legend. The Samguk sagi, a history of the early Korean kingdoms, has an entry under the nineteenth year of King Uija of Baekje, in the second month, which falls in 659. It says that several foxes came into the palace, and that one white fox sat on the desk of the sangjwapyeong, the highest-ranking minister (S5). The entry gives the fox's colour and the place it sat. It gives no tail count, no woman's shape, and no name. The animal is an omen, a creature in the wrong room, at the desk where the business of the state was done. Reference works list such fox reports among the signs recorded before the fall of Baekje and Goguryeo, and add that Korean texts speak of a nine-tailed fox by that count only from the Goryeo period onward, while the earlier accounts speak of white foxes (S6).

That gap matters. A reader who arrives from modern stories might expect the nine tails to be the old part of the figure. The record read here says the opposite: the white fox in the palace is old, and the nine tails are a later arrival. Where the tails came from is the subject of the next chapter, because the oldest nine-tailed fox in any East Asian text was not written in Korea at all.

What the Korean tales kept, and what they added, can be sorted into three layers. The first is the animal and the omen, shown by the white fox of 659. The second is the borrowed number and the borrowed shape, which came with Chinese books. The third is something the Korean tales made for themselves: a bead held in the fox's mouth, a young man who must decide where to look, and, much later, a fox that wishes to be human. The bead belongs to the Korean tales read for this account. It does not appear in the Chinese passage the next chapter reads.

There is also the question of how she is told. Reference works describe the Korean tradition as mostly hostile to foxes, with a fox spirit that deceives, with the tales that were told about her cast as warnings, and with a few minority tales in which a fox helps people (S6). Those tales often say that she preyed on people, and the account that follows will not dwell on it. The Korean sources used here are interested in other things: what a young man gives up, what he takes, and what he learns.

So the figure is a stack of layers, and each layer comes from a different place. A white fox on a minister's desk. A nine-tailed beast on a Chinese mountain. A bead passed between two mouths in a schoolroom or a field. The next chapter starts at the mountain.

Hook: The oldest nine-tailed fox in writing has no woman's shape, no bead and no schoolroom. What does it have?

#### ch-2 · The Oldest Nine Tails
anchor: gumiho--ch-2
sources: S3, S4, S7, S6
The Shanhaijing, the Classic of Mountains and Seas, is a Chinese collection of mountains, seas, peoples and beasts. Reference works date its compilation to the span between the Warring States period and the early Han dynasty (S7). The nine-tailed fox is first in its pages, and the text that carries it is short enough to hold in one hand.

In the section on the southern mountains, the entry for Qingqiu Mountain describes an animal shaped like a fox, with nine tails. Its cry sounds like a baby's (S3). The same entry marks the beast as a danger to people. It adds that anyone who eats its flesh is spared a certain affliction, so that the animal carries a protective sense beside the dangerous one. The entry gives a place, a shape, a sound, and these two uses. It gives no woman's form and no name for a spirit.

The second appearance is in the section on the lands beyond the eastern seas. There the text speaks of a country of Qingqiu, whose people eat the five grains and wear silk, and says of the foxes there that they have four legs and nine tails (S4). One page of the text records a variant reading that places this sentence differently, and another account puts the country in a different position relative to the Valley of the Morning Sun (S4). The foxes are part of the geography: animals that live in a place, like the people who live in the same place. They are described as unremarkable.

Compared with the Korean figure, these two passages leave out nearly everything. There is no bead. There is no young man, and no schoolroom. The fox is not said to grow older in order to reach nine tails, because the nine are simply how that species of fox is shaped. The sound of a baby's cry is the only hint of a lure.

How did the figure travel from this to a woman on a doorstep? The reference works used here offer a rough staircase. The fourth-century writer Guo Pu is reported as saying that foxes gain human form and magical powers with age, and that at a thousand years a fox ascends to heaven as a celestial fox (S7). In that reading the nine tails become a mark of a fox that has grown very old, where the Qingqiu text treats them as the shape of the species. Later fiction gave the fox a more menacing role. The famous nine-tailed fox Daji in the novel Investiture of the Gods was brought to Joseon in the seventeenth century, where she became known as Dalgi (S6), and a scholarly article cited by reference works places a fox demon sent to bring disaster to the Shang dynasty in fiction of the Yuan period (S7).

The meaning shifted, then, in steps. A beast on a mountain with a baby's cry. A fox of the east, one more animal among the grain-eating, silk-wearing people. An old fox with human form and a heavenly rank. A temptress in a novel that crossed the sea.

By the time such a fox reached Korean storytellers, they already had a fox of their own, a white one in a palace. The Chinese figure supplied the tails and the human shape. The Korean tales supplied the bead.

Hook: In a Korean tale a fox kisses a young man, and something small and bright passes between their mouths. What is it, and where must he look?

#### ch-3 · The Bead and the Sky
anchor: gumiho--ch-3
sources: S1, S6
The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, published by the Academy of Korean Studies, has an entry on the fox bead tale, the yeowu guseul seolhwa. It was written by the folklorist Cho Hee-woong in 1995 and last revised in 2023 (S1). The entry gives the tale several other names, among them "Gumiho and the Yeouiju" and "The Limits of Human Knowledge," and files it among the transformation tales of the marvellous kind. It is the clearest description of the tale read for this account.

The entry describes two main types. In the first, a young scholar at a village school falls in love with a beautiful woman who is really a fox. She will not kiss him. He threatens to end the relationship, and at that she gives in. When the kiss comes, a bead passes from her mouth into his, and he takes it, and so he holds in his mouth what the fox kept in hers. Then comes the part the tale was told for. The bead gives knowledge of what the holder looks at while it is in his mouth. The young man looks at the earth, not at the sky, and so he understands the affairs of the earth and little of heaven (S1).

[Dramatization] The moment itself is small. A woman and a young man stand close in the dark, so close that neither sees the lantern behind them. She tilts her face to him and he feels, between her mouth and his, something round and warm, no larger than a pea and glowing faintly. What the young man cannot yet know is which way to look, and the tale will say that this is the whole matter.

The entry presents this as an origin tale. The close of the story is also a statement about people: human beings know a great deal about the world under their feet and know little about the sky, because the first man to take the bead looked down. In the text of the entry the explanation is the whole point. The tale uses a fox, a bead and a kiss to answer a plain question about human knowledge, and that is why it carries the alternate name about the limits of what people can know.

The entry says the tale is found throughout Korea. Gumiho tales in general are reported to be especially common in three provinces, Hamgyeong, Pyeongan and Hwanghae (S1). A tale told so widely survives in many forms, and the entry notes one habit of the later forms, treated in the next chapter: the young man was often given the name of a real scholar from the local past.

Other writings take up the same bead. Reference works describe the tale of Jeon Woo-chi, in which a scholar is advised by his teacher to take the marble from a fox spirit's mouth, and in which he swallows it and gains supernatural powers (S6). They describe an oral tale in which the fox tries to kiss a man in order to draw off his vital energy, and he pretends to return the kiss, takes the marble, swallows it and gains divine knowledge (S6). They also mention an old novel, Myeonghaengjeonguirok, in which a fox forms an elixir that a woman swallows by accident, so that the fox's disguise is broken (S6). These accounts are known to the reference works rather than read at the source. They show the same object in different mouths: a bead in a fox, in a student, in a woman.

The bead in the Korean tales is not power. It is knowledge, and the tale is careful to say how much of it a man can hold. The question of what the fox wants from the young man is different in the second type, and the answer is larger than a kiss.

Hook: In the second type, a fox visits a schoolhouse by night and has a number in mind. How many sleepers does she need?

#### ch-4 · A Hundred Sleepers
anchor: gumiho--ch-4
sources: S1, S6
The same encyclopedia entry describes a second type of the fox bead tale, and this one gives the fox a purpose (S1). In it the fox wishes to ascend to heaven. To do so, the tale says, she must prey on a hundred students with a kiss, and nothing more needs to be told of how. She goes to a schoolhouse by night. She passes among the sleeping students and reaches ninety-nine of them. The hundredth is awake, or wakes in time, and slips away.

The rest keeps its shape from the first type, but the setting moves outside. The young man who woke follows her out into the night, away from the village. There she passes the bead again and again between her mouth and his, and the passing takes a toll on him. He grows weak, and the story lets him come close to the end of his strength. A schoolmaster, hearing of it, recognises her for what she is. He names her a gumiho and gives the student the advice that settles the tale: swallow the bead (S1). The young man does. From that point the story meets the first type, and the explanation of earthly and heavenly knowledge follows, because the swallowed bead gives what it gives to whoever looks at the earth.

The two types differ in a point easy to miss. In the first, the entry says nothing about what the fox wants. In the second, she wants heaven, not a human shape. The desire to become human, which dominates the modern image of the gumiho, is absent from both types in the entry. The fox in the bead tale reaches upward, to the sky, and the young man, by looking down, is the one who keeps his feet on the ground.

The entry records a habit of the later tellers. Over time the tale often became fastened to named historical figures in local legend. It was told of Jo Mok, a disciple of the scholar Yi Hwang, in the Andong area. It was told of Yun Seon-do in Haenam, South Jeolla, and of Yi Sik in Yangpyeong, Gyeonggi, and there were other attachments (S1). The entry does not say these men met a fox. It says the town told the tale of its own scholar, so that the legend belonged to the place. The name of the hero changed with the place, and the bead, the kiss and the look at the earth did not.

Why students? The setting offers a reason. A school at night, a student awake over his books, a teacher who knows what to do: the world of the tale is a world where knowledge is the valued prize. The bead gives knowledge, the fox wants to rise, and the young man who holds the bead is the one who studied. Whether the tellers meant this as a compliment to scholars or a joke on them is not in the entry. The entry marks the tale as an explanation of why people know what they know.

One more detail is easy to overlook. Nothing in the tale blames the young man. He is not a hero by strength or by cunning. He is the one who happened to be awake, who followed, who took the advice. The tale makes a small bright thing of luck and attention, and then closes on the sky.

Hook: A different tale never mentions a bead at all. It is about a family that wished for a daughter. What did they get?

#### ch-5 · The Fox Sister
anchor: gumiho--ch-5
sources: S2, S10, S6
The tale of the fox sister, yeou nuidongsaeng, is among the best known of the Korean fox tales, and it works in a different way. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture lists it among the human-transformation tales, in the disguise type: a fox is born into a family as the younger sister, and the youngest brother defeats her (S2). The entry written by Yu Jin-a in 2022 gives that much and no more. It does not say what the fox wants, and it names no number of victims. For the rest of the outline the reference works must be used, and their account is tagged as reported.

In the version summarised by reference works, a father who has three sons and no daughter prays for one, saying he would accept a fox. A girl is born. When she is six, the household's cattle begin to be lost, one a night, and the elder brothers raise the matter with their parents. The parents do not believe them, and the brothers are sent away. The youngest brother stays, and in time he is the one who ends the sister's hold on the house (S10). Other versions differ in how she is stopped, and a study of 2021 is reported to count at least seventy-five variants across the south of Korea (S10).

That outline is as far as this account goes. The tale has a hard core and the sources agree it is a tale about fear. One thesis, reported by reference works, reads the main themes as obsession and fear (S10). Its force lies in the home turned strange: the sister is at the table, and the family's animals and then the family itself are lost.

The fox sister is nonetheless useful for what it lacks. In the entry read here, her wish is not stated. In the first tale type of the bead story, there is no wish stated either. The idea that a fox wishes to be a human is relatively rare in the older Korean tales, according to reference works, which set it in a few Joseon-era stories: The Fox Sister, The Tale of Palbaek, The Fox Den and The Fox Wife (S6). One of them, the novel Myeonghaengjeonguirok, has a fox wait ten years for a human to give her a poem so that she may become human (S6). The reference works attribute the modern prominence of the wish to a reaction against the Joseon contempt for fox tales, to feminist aims of freeing women from the insult in the word, and to other folklore (S6).

Popular retellings add a rule that appears in many modern summaries: to become human, the fox must stay unseen for a set number of days, a hundred or a thousand, depending on who tells it. The sources read for this account do not record that rule. The encyclopedia entries do not give it, the fox sister outline has no such count, and the only counts found in the Korean tales are the hundred sleepers of the second bead type and the ninety-nine she reached. The set number of days therefore sits on the told side.

What the fox sister adds to the picture is the household. The bead tale is set in a school and a field, and the sister tale is set at home. The first is about what a young man may know, the second about whom a family can trust. Neither one asks the fox what she wants.

Hook: The Chinese, the Japanese and the Korean fox each carry a different thing. What does the Korean fox carry in her mouth that the others do not?

#### ch-6 · Cousins Abroad, and the Fox on Screen
anchor: gumiho--ch-6
sources: S6, S7, S8
China, Japan and Korea share the fox with the tails, and each settles it differently. In China, reference works report, the Song-era compilation Taiping Guangji gives the stages of a fox's growth: it can take another shape at fifty years, gains greater powers at a hundred, and becomes a heavenly fox at a thousand (S8). The fourth-century writer Guo Pu is reported to say the same about age and ascent (S7). The Chinese fox is mostly a creature of cultivation: the older she is, the more she can do.

In Japan the fox is the kitsune, and the nine-tailed form is called kyubi no kitsune. A reference work tells that it reached Japan along with Chinese tales, and that the famous nine-tailed fox Tamamo-no-Mae is said to descend from the Chinese Daji (S8). Folktales hold that a fox grows an extra tail after a hundred years, and that kitsune have up to nine tails (S8). The fox as seductress, the reference work adds, is traced to Chinese stories, though some Japanese elements are unique (S8). Reference works add that Japanese tradition took the fox in positively, a synthesis for which Korea shows no counterpart (S6). Reference works describe the Korean fox as mostly an adversary, and the Japanese fox as often a helper.

Set side by side, the three foxes carry different things. The Chinese fox carries age and rank. The Japanese fox carries tails counted in centuries. The Korean tales read for this account give her a bead in the mouth, and the bead gives knowledge. Reference works describe the Korean tradition as one that holds that a young man swallowing a fox's bead gains wisdom or divine knowledge, and no such tale was found in the Chinese passage of the previous chapters (S6). Reference works treat the bead as the Korean part of the figure. The nine tails are the shared part.

There is also a quiet change in what the nine tails mean from country to country. In the oldest Chinese text they are the fox's species. In Chinese commentary they are a sign of age. In the Japanese folktales they are earned slowly, a tail every hundred years. In Korean use the word gumiho slides from the novels to cover all the fox spirits of older writing, so that the nine tails have become a name for the whole family of foxes (S6).

The modern image is the last turn. In roughly the past forty years, Korean film and television have shown the gumiho as a lovelorn or tragic figure who longs to be human, and reference works trace the shift to the same reaction against older contempt for fox tales (S6). The figure in these stories is an adaptation, with fixed designs of its own, and those designs are not the folk figure, which never had a fixed look. The folk gumiho is a shadow on a paper door.

The record is clearest where it is a page. A white fox in a palace year. A mountain in a Chinese classic. An encyclopedia's two types of bead tale. The next chapter sorts the pages from the telling.

Hook: Which parts of the gumiho can be pointed to on a page, and which are only repeated?

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: gumiho--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10
The record is clear in a few places. The Shanhaijing describes an animal shaped like a fox, with nine tails and a baby's cry, on Qingqiu Mountain, and says the foxes of the country of Qingqiu have four legs and nine tails (S3, S4). The Samguk sagi records that several foxes entered the Baekje palace in the nineteenth year of King Uija, 659, and that one white fox sat on the desk of the highest minister (S5). The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture records the fox bead tale in two types, one in which the young man looks at the earth and not the sky, and one in which the fox needs a hundred students and the schoolmaster advises swallowing the bead (S1). The same encyclopedia lists the fox sister among the disguise-type transformation tales, with the youngest brother as the one who overcomes her (S2).

Some things sit between the columns. Most of the comparison with China and Japan, the staircase of Guo Pu, Daji and the Taiping Guangji, the Joseon-era novels, the claim that Korean texts name a nine-tailed fox only from the Goryeo period, and the modern turn of the figure come from reference works that cite scholars the sources read here did not reach (S6, S7, S8). The seventy-five variants and the fox sister's outline come from a reference page as well (S10). The coordinates of the museum come from a reference page (S9).

The told side is large. That the white fox of 659 was a nine-tailed gumiho is not in the text, which gives no tails. That a fox must stay unseen for a set number of days to become human is a popular rule, and none of the sources read records it. That the fox in the bead tales wants to be human is not in the entry, where the second type wants heaven. That the bead holds the knowledge of the sky is the explanation the tale gives, and it is a story and not a finding. That the bead tale truly happened to Jo Mok, Yun Seon-do or Yi Sik is a claim of local legend. That the nine tails always meant a seductress is wrong even in the oldest text, where they are the shape of a species.

The rule is the same as for any story kept on paper. A claim that can be read on a page can be sourced. A claim read only in the telling stays on the told side, however often it is repeated.

A note on method. The Chinese passages were read in the classical text as published on Wikisource and are paraphrased. The Korean entries were read in the Academy of Korean Studies online encyclopedia. Where a reference work and a primary page differ, the primary page was given the weight. No quotation exceeds a few words.

What survives is a short list of things that hold the figure together: a white fox at a minister's desk, a mountain with a baby's cry, a bead passed from one mouth to another, a look at the earth where the sky was meant, and a family that wished for a daughter. Take away the nine tails and the tales still stand. Take away the bead and the figure becomes a fox that learned to be a woman. On the paper door the shadow keeps its shape, and the nine strokes behind it do not quite hold still.

Hook: On the paper door the shadow bows its head, and the lantern outside does not flicker.

**v-bead**
anchor: gumiho--versions--v-bead
In this account a young scholar at a village school loves a woman who is a fox. She refuses to kiss him. He tells her that he will end the relationship, and she gives way. When their mouths meet, the bead passes from hers into his, and it is his to keep. The bead gives knowledge of whatever the holder looks upon, and the young man looks at the earth, not at the sky. So he knows the earth's affairs and little of heaven, and the tale closes by saying that this is why all people are the same. The refusal, the threat to leave and the downward look belong to this account. No count of victims and no wish of the fox's is told in it.

**v-ascend**
anchor: gumiho--versions--v-ascend
In this account the fox wants to rise to heaven and needs a hundred students. She enters a schoolhouse by night and reaches ninety-nine sleepers. The hundredth, awake or woken, escapes and follows her outside the village. Between her mouth and his, the bead passes back and forth, and he grows weak. A schoolmaster recognises her as a gumiho and advises him to swallow the bead. After that the story is the same as the first account. The hundred, the ninety-nine and the schoolmaster's advice belong to this account. Her goal is the sky, not a human face.

**v-sister**
anchor: gumiho--versions--v-sister
In this account the fox arrives in a house as a daughter. A father with three sons has asked for a girl even if she be a fox, and a girl is born. At six she is a child of the house, and the household's animals begin to be lost, one a night. The elder brothers see where the trouble lies and are not believed. The youngest brother, who stays, is the one who in the end overcomes her. The household, the three sons and the lost animals belong to this account. It has no bead and no schoolroom, and no wish is stated.

**v-qingqiu**
anchor: gumiho--versions--v-qingqiu
In this account the nine-tailed fox is a feature of the geography of the Chinese classic. On Qingqiu Mountain a beast is shaped like a fox, with nine tails and a baby's cry, and it is dangerous to people. Anyone who eats its flesh is spared a certain affliction. In the section of lands to the east, the country of Qingqiu has foxes with four legs and nine tails, and its people eat the five grains and wear silk. The baby's cry, the four legs and the protective flesh belong to this account. There is no human form in it.

**v-local**
anchor: gumiho--versions--v-local
In this account the bead tale happened in a particular district to a particular scholar. In the Andong area he is Jo Mok, a disciple of Yi Hwang. In Haenam in South Jeolla he is Yun Seon-do, and in Yangpyeong in Gyeonggi he is Yi Sik. The kiss, the bead and the downward look are the same, and the name of the scholar and the place are what change. The district's own scholar belongs to this account. The type entries treat the tale as nameless, and no record read shows that any of these men met a fox.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands in the courtyard of a hanok at night, with a lantern, paper doors and a stone wall. From there, a footbridge over a stream leads out through gold reeds, and past it a mountain forest rises under a full moon. A woman's shadow rests on one of the paper doors.

**Scenes**

- `scene-courtyard` (the courtyard): From left to right, a hanok wall with a row of paper doors, a woman's shadow on one door with faint tail shapes in it, a low writing desk with a book box and a brush, an earthen jar, a stone step, a hanging lantern, a courtyard stone wall with a gate. Mood: still, watched, intimate. Light: one paper lantern, warm amber, glowing through the doors.
- `scene-bridge` (the bridge): From left to right, a stretch of stone wall, autumn reeds at the stream's edge, a small stone bridge over the stream, a flat river stone, the far bank with a path rising, a bare pine bough over the water. Mood: eerie, calm, vast. Light: the full moon and the soft glow of the bead.

**Breaking the curse:** The final puzzle is the shadow's last question. The visitor chooses from a row of four tokens (a glowing bead, a ring of nine tails, a paper-door frame and a small bundle of reeds) the one that matches the true account: in v-bead the glowing bead; in v-ascend the ring of nine tails counted to ninety-nine marks; in v-sister the paper-door frame; in v-qingqiu the bundle of reeds standing for the Qingqiu text. v-local never matches. A right answer lets the shadow fold into the grain of the paper door as a single calm outline. The relic earned is a small glass bead lantern: a clear bead in a wire cradle that gives a soft jade-white light when held up to the moon.

4. The reeds at the bridge hiss once with no wind.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Folklore, not religion. No shaman rite, temple, monk or exorcism drives any chapter. A monk who in some versions of the fox sister gives the brothers magic items is left out. A saint-like or religious reading of the bead is not used.
- The gumiho is described in tales as preying on people. The account says this in one neutral clause at most and never shows or describes harm, organs or blood. The fox sister is told as an outline only; animals and family members are "lost". Nothing in any image shows a victim.
- The bead is a kiss tale in the sources; it is kept to a mouth-to-mouth passing of a bead with no further detail.
- The gumiho here is an original design: a pale fox silhouette with nine tails in moonlight, or a woman's shadow on a paper door with tail shapes. Do not copy any film, TV, comic or game gumiho, including "My Girlfriend Is a Gumiho", "Tale of the Nine Tailed", "Gu Family Book", the League of Legends character Ahri, Naruto's nine-tailed fox, any soju brand mascot or any mascot of a Korean broadcaster.
- Real people are named only as places in local legend (Jo Mok, Yun Seon-do, Yi Sik) and as authors of reference entries (Cho Hee-woong, Yu Jin-a). No quote or deed is invented for any of them. The v-local version is never a true account.
- Items not checked against a primary text (tagged [R], never used in true-or-told slots): the compilation span of the Shanhaijing, the staircase of Guo Pu and Daji, the Taiping Guangji stages, the Japanese tail counts, the Goryeo-onward claim, the fox sister outline, the seventy-five variants and the museum coordinates.
- Claims left out as unconfirmed: a Goguryeo king's white fox omen; Guo Pu's reading of the fox as a sign of peace; the Korean comparison article claiming the bead tale exists only in Korea; the Chinese "monster sister" comparison; the Yuan-period fox demon article was read only through a reference page.
- The National Folk Museum was not checked for any gumiho object; the pin marks the museum and nothing more.
