## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] A kitchen radio is on low in a farmhouse in the hills of Puerto Rico, in the autumn of 1995. A host is talking about the animals that have been found dead on farms across the island, and he is joking and serious in the same breath. He says a word that is two words stuck together, and the word is so easy to say that the whole kitchen repeats it. Nobody in the room has seen anything. By the next morning, the word is on a hundred lips, and farmers are checking their pens before breakfast. A name has arrived before the creature has.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · The Vampire Before the Name
anchor: chupacabra--ch-1
sources: S3, S8
The chupacabra is often described as a creature with no past, a monster that appeared in the spring and summer of 1995. The record is more careful than that. Writers who study the legend point to an earlier Puerto Rican story, a "vampire of Moca", from 1975. Moca is a municipality in Puerto Rico, and in the telling that survives, a run of unexplained livestock deaths there was blamed on a mysterious attacker (S8). Skeptoid's summary of the story adds a detail: the 1975 creature was imagined as a giant, winged and feathered being, which is not the figure of 1995 at all (S3).

That difference matters. A vampire in a mountain town and a spiny biped on a village street are not the same monster, and the later legend borrowed the earlier one's mood more than its shape. Both stories grew from a common soil. Benjamin Radford, a skeptical investigator who spent about five years on the subject, researched Latin American and Puerto Rican vampire folklore to understand the cultural setting from which the later figure emerged (S1). He also noted that the chupacabra is almost exclusively reported in Spanish-speaking countries and regions, which he treats as a sign of a strong cultural element (S1).

When an animal is found dead in the morning and nobody saw why, people look for an explanation, and an explanation that has a face is easier to carry than one that has none. Reports of this kind, in which animals were said to have been "drained" of blood, circulated in the United States in the same decades under the name "cattle mutilations", and Radford says the question of what appears to drain blood from livestock was answered long ago in that context (S1). Natural decay and ordinary predation, he writes, can look unusual to an untrained eye, and a claim of total blood loss cannot be checked without a proper necropsy (S1).

So by 1995 several ingredients were already in place on the island: a vampire in recent local memory, a long tradition of fearing for animals, and a press and radio that could carry a story quickly. What was missing was a trigger and a shape. The legend would need a name, a description and a way to move.

It is also worth noticing what the Moca story shows about how legends are remembered. The 1975 vampire is mostly known today because writers in the 1990s went looking for precedents, and some cite it as proof that the chupacabra is older than 1995 (S3). Others note that the figure of 1995 does not match it. The 1975 Moca vampire was the same creature as the 1995 one. That link is told only, a connection made by later writers and not by the 1975 reports (S3). A later legend can reach back and adopt an earlier one as its ancestor without the earlier one ever having been described the same way. The past of a modern legend is often built after the fact.

In March 1995, the first reports that would later be counted as part of the story arrived, and they were about animals, not about a monster.

Hook: A farmer in the hills of central Puerto Rico finds something wrong in the pen one morning. What was reported, and who was asked to explain it?

#### ch-2 · Eight Sheep in March
anchor: chupacabra--ch-2
sources: S3, S1, S7, S8
The reports that began the chupacabra story are usually placed in March 1995. A summary of the early record says that eight sheep were found dead on a farm, and that the reports said they had been drained of blood (S7, reported). The place is not agreed. Skeptoid places the livestock deaths of 1995 in Orocovis and Morovis, municipalities in the central mountains of the island (S3). Other popular accounts give different towns, and the sources used here do not settle the matter between them. The livestock were found dead, and that is where the firm part of the account ends.

What is firm is the atmosphere. Radford writes that the early accounts were full of confusion and contradiction, and he quotes a researcher who found it almost impossible to separate fact from hearsay and local lore (S1). The reports travelled by word of mouth, by telephone and through the press, and each retelling made the detail a little sharper. A farm where something had happened became a district where something was happening.

Official voices were pulled in. A veterinarian named in a reference summary, David Morales, is said to have examined hundreds of the animals reported in the island's wave and to have found that they had not been bled dry (S8, reported). That claim is given here as reported only. What can be said from Radford's own text is that very few of the animals described as "completely bloodless" were ever examined by professionals, and that a forensic pathologist he interviewed told him that blood loss cannot be confirmed without a necropsy (S1).

In other words, the phrase "drained of blood" was in circulation before it had been tested. It entered the story as a rumour and quickly became part of the monster's definition. The word that did most of the work was a way of describing what people feared. The animals were drained of blood. That claim is told only: no necropsy ever confirmed it (S1).

Seen from outside, this is the first step in how a modern legend forms in real time. A cluster of events is noticed. A pattern is claimed. A feature that sounds distinctive is attached to the pattern, and the feature then guides what people look for next. Farmers who had heard that the animals were "drained" checked for that sign, and any dead animal could now be read through it.

In the months that followed, the number of reported animals grew. By August one account counts as many as 150 animals said to have been killed in the same way, though the sources used here give that as a claim and not as a count (S8, S4). The first stage of the story was an island talking to itself.

Skeptoid adds that for about five months the chupacabra was discussed almost entirely inside Puerto Rico, among people interested in UFOs and vampires (S3). That matches what Radford describes: a local story with an audience of believers, not yet a national figure and not yet a monster with a name. A name and an image would change that.

The image came from a single description that has since been cited more than any other.

Hook: In the second week of August, a witness on a village street says she saw the creature itself, and her description is about to be cited more than any other.

#### ch-3 · A Description from Canóvanas
anchor: chupacabra--ch-3
sources: S1, S3, S7
Canóvanas is a municipality east of San Juan, and it is where the most famous chupacabra description comes from. According to Radford, a woman who lived there reported seeing the creature near the street outside her house during the second week of August 1995 (S1). The Canóvanas description was published in a 1997 book by Scott Corrales, Chupacabras and Other Mysteries (S1). She is a private person, and she is not named here.

The description, as Radford summarises it, was very specific. The creature was a biped, roughly four feet tall, with thin arms and legs and three fingers or toes at the end of each. It had no ears and no nose, only two small airholes. Its dark eyes were large and seemed to run up the temples and spread around the sides of the head. A row of long spikes ran down its back (S1). The description of the creature was an upright biped with spikes. This is documented as a description (Corrales, 1997) and nothing more (S1). Skeptoid's retelling gives a smaller figure, about three feet tall, that hopped like a kangaroo, and says the larger heights are a common misreport (S3). In later tellings the spikes became the single most repeated detail of the monster, and the picture most people have in mind, drawn in countless books and magazines, descends from this description (S1).

Skeptoid places the sighting in the yard of the witness's mother, around 54 km east of the livestock reports of Orocovis and Morovis, and says her account spread widely and shaped much of the legend (S3). The distance is an important detail. The farm deaths in the hills and the sighting on a village street were two separate stories in two separate places. They became one story because someone, or something, joined them.

A reported public reaction followed. A summary of the story says that the mayor of Canóvanas at the time, José Soto, recruited volunteers to hunt the creature and that they found nothing (S7, reported). The mayor is a public official and the detail is given as reported. If it is accurate, it shows the legend entering civic life: a town's leader gave the rumour the authority of a search party.

The description was also the source of a long-running question that Radford set out to answer. If the creature were an animal, it was an unfamiliar one. A biped with spikes, no nose and three-fingered hands does not resemble any known species. Radford says he interviewed the witness at length, visited the place where she said it happened, and concluded that her account contained implausible observations, contradictions and inconsistencies (S1). He argues that because this description is the "original" eyewitness account, the one on which most images of the creature are based, any weakness in it tends to run through much of what followed (S1).

This is one researcher's reading, and it is not shared by everyone who writes about the subject. Others stress that nobody can say what the witness saw, only what she said she saw. The documented part is the report and its date. The interpretation belongs to those who have examined it.

Nonetheless, a question was now sharp enough to be asked. The creature described had no known animal counterpart. Where might the picture have come from? Radford asked what, in Puerto Rico in the summer of 1995, could have supplied a shape like that.

Hook: Radford went looking for something that arrived on the island just before the sighting, and he found a film that he connects to the description.

#### ch-4 · One Researcher's Argument
anchor: chupacabra--ch-4
sources: S1, S3, S4
In the Skeptical Inquirer for May and June 2011, Benjamin Radford set out how he believes the original description came about (S1). The argument is his own, and it is presented here as one researcher's documented view, not as settled fact. Benjamin Radford argued that the description matches the creature in the film Species. Radford presents the film link as his own argument about the description. It is documented as one researcher's view (S1).

Radford says he looked for anything new that had entered Puerto Rican popular culture shortly before the August 1995 sighting. He found a film. The science-fiction horror film Species, he writes, was released in Puerto Rico on 7 July 1995, just over a month before the report from Canóvanas (S1). In the film an alien creature called Sil appears, and Radford says that Sil has more than a dozen features in common with the witness's description. He lists a large, oblong head, large wraparound eyes, a small or missing nose, no ears, an upright stance, long thin limbs, clawed fingers, a small mouth, large spikes down the spine and no tail (S1).

He adds a statement from the witness herself, as published by Corrales in 1997: she is quoted as saying that the resemblance between the film's creature and the chupacabra was really impressive (S1). Radford takes this as support for his reading. He also notes that witnesses of other monsters have described creatures they saw in films, and he calls that possible, not proven (S1).

His conclusion is that the original description was based on a science-fiction film. This was a hypothesis built on timing and resemblance. It does not claim that the witness lied, only that she may have described an image already in her head. Other writers have raised questions about it. One notes that it does not explain why the monster took the name it did, which is a separate matter (S4). Another debate is whether the witness saw the film before or after her sighting, a point on which the sources used here only repeat what Radford was told (S1).

The argument is useful because it shows how a modern legend can take shape in a media-rich place. A film's creature reaches hundreds of thousands of viewers. A frightening evening, a local rumour about dead animals and a half-remembered image meet in one person's account. A newspaper or a radio host then carries that account to everyone else.

It also helps explain a feature that troubled researchers: the creature had its odd spiny shape only in Puerto Rico. When the chupacabra was reported elsewhere, Radford observes, it took on a very different form (S1). In some countries, a few of the dead animals found turned out to be small four-legged members of the dog family, such as dogs and coyotes (S1). The spiny biped was one image, and the four-legged animal was another, and the same word was used for both.

The word had been spoken before the film argument was ever made. By the end of 1995, it was on the radio.

Hook: Who first put the two words together, and how sure are the sources that it was him?

#### ch-5 · A Name on the Radio, and Over the Borders
anchor: chupacabra--ch-5
sources: S3, S4, S1, S6, S8
"Chupacabras" means "goat sucker" in Spanish. The name is generally credited to the comedian and radio personality Silverio Pérez, a Puerto Rican who spoke about the livestock deaths on his programme in San Juan in 1995 (S3, S8). He is a public entertainer, and the credit is widely repeated. The credit is not, however, firm. Jason Colavito, writing about the prehistory of the word, accepts that Pérez may have been the first to apply it to the creature before the end of 1995, but argues that he did not invent the term, which belongs to older goat-sucker traditions in Europe and America (S4). He traces it to a small, harmless bird: the nightjar, long called a goatsucker in Europe, and known in Puerto Rico as the chotacabra (S4). Skeptoid adds that some researchers have found earlier uses of the compound word, though 1995 Puerto Rico is the first known use of it as a monster's name (S3). The attribution, in short, is a probable first use, not a proven invention. Earlier uses of the compound word are known, so the credit is not firm. That is documented (S3, S4).

The name worked because it was easy to say and easy to picture. It explained the deaths in one word, and it gave a face to what had been only a rumour. Radio in the island's kitchens and cars carried it faster than any newspaper could. Newspapers and television then took up the story and repeated both name and description, and the two stuck together. Radford writes that for about five years after 1995 the creature was widely reported, and that by 2002 it was known worldwide. He quotes a writer who said that not since crop circles had a strange phenomenon been so quickly absorbed into popular culture (S1).

The spread followed the Spanish-speaking world. Radford lists Mexico, Chile, Nicaragua, Spain, Argentina and Florida among the places where sightings were reported in the legend's early years (S1). In Mexico, a report places at least thirty sheep found dead overnight in a mountain municipality in Puebla in 1996. State news outlets reported it first and national media picked up the story quickly, and it became one of the best-known Mexican legends (S6, reported). A popular summary also places an episode in the Miami area in 1996, in which dozens of small farm animals were reported dead in a single night and police and a zoologist pointed to a large dog (S8, reported). These two accounts are reported only and have not been checked against the original press.

What the sources do agree on is the mechanism. In each place the word arrived first. People who had heard it were ready to apply it to local losses, and the local losses then seemed to confirm the word. Benjamin Radford wrote that the early witnesses saw wildly different things and called them all the same name (S1).

That explains why the chupacabra of Mexico, Chile and Florida did not look like the one from Canóvanas. The label travelled and the picture changed with each region. The word carried the legend; the description did not.

Radford also points to how swiftly the story moved because each new sighting was reported in the news almost as soon as it happened, so that a sighting in one country became the next country's evidence (S1). In effect, the media were a part of the phenomenon, not just a record of it.

By the mid-2000s, the name had crossed the border into the United States in earnest, and there it found a new animal.

Hook: On a dry road in Texas, ranchers began finding something hairless and thin, and scientists soon named the animal.

#### ch-6 · The Hairless Animal on the Dry Road
anchor: chupacabra--ch-6
sources: S1, S2, S5, S8
From the mid-2000s, a second kind of chupacabra began to appear in Texas. It was not a biped with spikes. Radford describes it as a canid form, reported mostly in Texas since the mid-2000s (S1). It was a small four-legged animal that looked oddly bare, thin and grey, with a long snout and sometimes a ridge along the back.

The best-known case is the animal found near Cuero, Texas, in 2007. A rancher found the carcass, and the case drew national attention (S1). Biologists at Texas State University tested a tissue sample. Texas State University reported on 2 November 2007 that the Cuero animal's DNA matched a coyote, Canis latrans (S2). The Texas State biologist quoted on the result was Mike Forstner of the biology department, who said the DNA was a virtually identical match (S2). The Texas State DNA result matched a coyote. That result is documented in the university's report (S2). The report adds that the animal may have had mange or another illness or disorder (S2). Radford, who travelled to Texas to examine the carcass, includes it among the canine cases that turned out to be ordinary dogs and coyotes (S1).

The explanation most often given by wildlife specialists is mange. John Tomeček, a Texas A&M wildlife specialist, links the look of the animals to late-stage mange (S5). He says that most reports likely involve a coyote in the late stages of mange, a skin disease caused by mites (S5). The disease causes hair loss, sores and thickened skin. Tomeček notes that the ridge between the shoulder blades is often the last area to lose its fur, which he links to the reports of spines (S5). Badly affected animals become very thin and can act unusually, for example approaching homes in search of food, and a bold animal is not necessarily an aggressive one (S5). He adds that most livestock reported as chupacabra losses involve small animals kept in pens or corrals, a situation that suits a weak and hungry predator (S5).

Other Texas reports followed the same pattern. A popular reference summary lists a 2004 case near San Antonio in which an animal was identified as a coyote with mange, and a 2010 case in Hood County in which Texas A&M identified a hybrid of a coyote and a dog with mange and parasites (S8, reported). A university biologist at Michigan is also reported to have found that United States cases fit mange-affected coyotes (S8, reported). These are reported only here.

What the Texas animals show is how a legend adapts. The original description did not match what was found on the road, so the legend changed. The "chupacabra" became whatever a frightened or curious person photographed that looked wrong. Radford observes that the monster has a strong cultural element and is almost exclusively seen in Spanish-speaking regions, which makes the Texas cases, in a region with a large Spanish-speaking population, a natural place for the name to land (S1).

Modern culture has kept the creature alive. It appears in books, television shows, souvenirs and cartoons, and each one has its own design. The designs differ because the record gives only a few details, not a single picture.

The creature is now a shared word more than a single animal. It names an idea: something unexplained at the edge of the farm, at night.

Hook: Some of the story stands on firm ground, and some has only ever been repeated.

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: chupacabra--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8
A legend that formed in the age of radio and print leaves a trail, and the trail shows where the facts stop.

On the documented side, livestock were reported dead in Puerto Rico in 1995, and the reports were widely repeated (S1, S3). A witness in Canóvanas gave a detailed description of a spiny, upright creature in the second week of August 1995, and her account was published by Scott Corrales in 1997 (S1). Radford reports that the film Species was released in Puerto Rico on 7 July 1995, and that the description shares more than a dozen features with the film's creature (S1). The name "chupacabras" was applied to the creature in 1995 and is generally credited to Silverio Pérez, with the caveat that earlier uses of the compound exist (S3, S4). In 2007 Texas State University biologists reported that the DNA from the Cuero animal matched a coyote (S2). A Texas A&M wildlife specialist explains the hairless look with late-stage mange (S5).

Some details sit between the columns. The March 1995 report of eight sheep, the figure of 150 animals, the actions of the mayor of Canóvanas, the Puebla and Miami episodes of 1996, and the Texas cases other than Cuero have been found only through reference pages and summaries. They are reported, not confirmed (S6, S7, S8).

On the told side, the claim that dead animals had been drained of blood has not been confirmed by examination. Radford stresses that few were examined and that such a claim requires a necropsy (S1). The 1975 vampire of Moca as an ancestor of the chupacabra is a connection made by later writers, not by the 1975 reports. A secret laboratory or government programme made the creature. That is told only: it is a rumour with no record behind it, and it concerns real institutions, so it can never be treated as a true account. The creature is an unknown species or an alien. That is told only: it belongs to belief, not to evidence. That a single hairless animal in Texas proves the Canóvanas creature was a coyote is not documented either: the Texas animals show what the name later meant, not what was seen in 1995.

On method: the sources disagree about small things, including the town of the first livestock reports, the exact day of the radio remark, and how much weight to give the film argument. Where they disagree, each is given with its source, and none is called correct. Radford's film argument is presented as his documented view. It is a strong explanation of the shape of the description, and it is not a proof of what any witness saw.

What survives is a pattern more than a creature. A cluster of dead animals, one striking description, a catchy name and a media world ready to carry them turned an island rumour into a legend in a matter of months. The legend then changed shape as it travelled, from a spiny biped in Puerto Rico to a bare, thin canine in Texas.

Hook: Somewhere beyond the last farm fence, a spiny shape stands half-hidden in the dark, and a radio is still talking.

## 7. Game world

A spiny, shadowy shape stays half-hidden in the foliage, always out of reach.

**Spirit encounter:** The creature never comes near. Questions arrive through the radio and the newspaper stand as short crackles and folded slips, each ending in a question about what was said and when. A wrong answer makes the foliage rustle and the fear meter rise with a low distant thump. A right answer pushes back a patch of dark and the silhouette steps further away. At the end it slips into the brush and the radio falls quiet.

4. A low shape crosses the far brush on the Texas road and is gone.
5. The edges of the screen darken briefly and clear.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Animal deaths are stated in one neutral line and the word "drained" appears only as what reports claimed. No wounds, blood or suffering are described, and no art shows any injured or distressed animal.
- The Canóvanas witness is a private person and is not named. Public figures are named only where the sources do so: Silverio Pérez (entertainer), José Soto (mayor, reported), authors and researchers.
- The Species film is named only as part of Radford's argument. Do not copy the creature from the film, the poster, or any film, TV, cartoon, comic or game chupacabra. The spirit in this file is an original silhouette with no alien head, no oversized wraparound eyes and no glossy skin.
- The v-lab version (a secret laboratory or government programme) points at real institutions without evidence. It is never a true version; its lead is always a dead end.
- Radford's film argument is one researcher's documented view and is not proof about what any witness saw. Other writers dispute parts of it (S4).
- Map point is the town of Canóvanas, a public municipality. Do not suggest visiting farms, hillsides, ranches or roads at night.
- Items not checked against an original page (tagged [R], never used in true-or-told slots): the March 1995 eight-sheep report, the mayor's volunteer hunt, the Puebla 1996 and Miami 1996 episodes. The map coordinates were not read from a fetched page.
