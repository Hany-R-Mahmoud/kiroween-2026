## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] Night watch. The sea is flat, the sails hang, and nobody speaks. Then the lookout points, and everyone on deck sees it: a small ship sailing at an easy pace across water that has no wind to give. Its sails are full. Yours are slack. A faint reddish light sits around its masts, and no lantern is lit on it. You count the heartbeats. The ship does not turn toward you, and it does not turn away. It simply crosses your bows and goes on, as if it had a place to be that no chart records. In the morning, the officers will argue about what they saw. Thirteen people will agree that they saw something. Nobody will agree on who sailed her, or why she is still out there.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · The Road Round the Cape
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-1
sources: S9, S1, S2, S11

[Dramatization] Picture a Dutch merchant ship in the seventeenth century, months out from home, her water casks sour and her crew counting days. Every sailor aboard has the same thought. Get to the Cape. Take on fresh food. Sleep one night on a deck that does not move.

That thought is the root of the legend, and it is easy to miss. The Flying Dutchman is a story about a ship that cannot reach port. It could only grow on a route where reaching port was the entire point.

The documented part is plain. In 1652 the Dutch East India Company, the VOC, set up a refreshment station at Table Bay under Jan van Riebeeck. Its job was to resupply ships sailing between the Netherlands and Batavia, in what is now Indonesia. The official name was a waystation. Sailors knew it more simply as the place where the long voyage either went well or went wrong. Ships arrived hungry. They left stocked. Between the two lay a headland that every crew of the age talked about in lowered voices.

Now add the second ingredient, the Dutch Golden Age itself. Trade ran on schedules, contracts and letters. Cargo was insured. A captain who was late owed an explanation to people who counted money for a living. A ship that did not arrive was not only a tragedy. It was an unpaid bill. So the idea of a captain bound never to arrive, never to unload and never to report, had a cold logic that sailors and merchants could both feel.

Here the legend begins to split into versions, and the atlas follows the splits. Some told it as a ship refused help at the Cape. Some told it as a captain who made an oath. Some told it as a gambler at his table. Some told it as a man waiting for a woman to keep a promise. Every version agrees on one thing: the ship cannot rest, and when it is seen, bad weather or worse is on the way.

Keep that last point in mind, because it travels. Sailors who had never seen the Cape carried the omen to other oceans. By the time the story reached print, it belonged to everyone who had ever stood a night watch and been afraid of what the dark would hand over.

It is worth stopping on one more detail. The legend is rarely tied to one named, dated wreck. No harbour record, no ship's roll and no inquiry ever gave the Dutchman a hull number. That does not make it dull. It makes it portable. A ship without a record can be anywhere, and a captain without a name can be anyone. Later writers gave him names. Early sailors mostly did not need to.

So where does the first trace appear? Not in a Dutch archive. The earliest known mention in print is a traveller's book published in 1790, by an author named John MacDonald. He did not write about a captain at all. He wrote about sailors, a storm, and a ship they said was the Flying Dutchman, one that had come to the Cape in bad weather, could not get a pilot to guide it into harbour, and was lost. That is all. Five years later, a second book would add a drowned warship and a ghost in full sail, and the legend would begin to sound like a story.

There is a quiet lesson here for anyone who reads the atlas closely: early texts say little and later texts say a great deal. The more detailed an account is, the later it tends to be. Remember that when two witnesses disagree.

Hook: The first printed account never names a captain, never names a curse, and never names a cause. Who gave him the name Vanderdecken, and why does a ship's crew in that story carry letters?

#### ch-2 · The First Whispers, 1790 to 1813
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-2
sources: S1, S2, S11, S12, S13, S14

Open the oldest pages and what you find is thinner than the legend you may have heard. In 1790, a travel book by John MacDonald mentions sailors who blamed the stormy weather on a ship they called the Flying Dutchman. According to the account, she had come to the Cape in bad weather, wanted to get into harbour, could not get a pilot to guide her, and was lost. That is the entire seed: a ship seeking help, and not receiving it.

Five years later, in 1795, a book credited to George Barrington, A Voyage to New South Wales, added a second layer. A Dutch warship is lost off the Cape with every soul aboard. Some time after, on the night watch of another ship in the same latitude, men see a vessel under full sail standing straight for them, and a frightened boatswain wakes the narrator to say it is the ghost. The story spreads from port to port, and the phantom gets its name. Here, for the first time, the ghost is something that was once a real ship and a real loss. Modern readers should handle this source with care, because scholars suspect that Barrington's books are literary forgeries. It is a printed account, and it was widely read. It is not evidence that a wreck happened.

Notice what these two tellers do. They do not explain the curse. They describe a condition: a ship that sails when it should not, appears when storms come, and cannot get what it needs. Call this the sailors' version. It is the plainest one, and the one with the fewest dramatic details. It is also the one that is hardest to catch out, for the same reason. A story with few details has few ways to be wrong.

Then writers start to improve on it. In 1803 the poet John Leyden published Scenes of Infancy. A note there links the ghost ship to hurricanes off the coast of Africa and gives a reason: a crime, a plague, and ports that would not take the crew in. In 1806 Thomas Moore published a poem that moved the ghost ship across the Atlantic to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and turned it into a silent vessel with a skeletal crew. In 1813 Walter Scott's notes to his poem Rokeby described a ship loaded with wealth, where murder and piracy were done, her crew struck down by plague and shut out of every harbour. Scott also wrote that sailors count the sight the worst of all possible omens, and he noted that his late friend Leyden had given the judgment a different cause, the first slave ship.

Put those side by side. In about twenty-three years, the cause changes at least four times. A missing pilot. A drowned warship. A plague ship that carried the first slave cargo. A plague ship of murderers and pirates with stolen wealth. The ship stays the same and the reason is swapped out each time the storyteller needs a new fear.

One more detail of the period matters. Naval and merchant crews were exactly the people who passed these stories along. They wrote letters, kept logs and swapped yarns in ports. A story spreading between ports has to be short enough to repeat and strange enough to remember. Both are true of the early Dutchman. Both change when someone writes it down for readers on land who have never stood a night watch.

By 1813 the ghost ship is famous enough to appear in notes to a popular poem. The ship has gone from a rumour about bad weather to a stock figure of English-language literature. What it needs now is a full story: a captain, a place, a message, and an ending. Eight years later, it gets one.

Hook: In May 1821 a Scottish magazine printed a tale with a named captain, a boat rowing across a storm, and a stranger holding a bundle of letters. The sailors who watched him knew every address on them was gone.

#### ch-3 · Vanderdecken's Message Home
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-3
sources: S1, S2, S15, S16

This chapter is about letters, and the letters are where the dread lives.

In May 1821, Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine published a story called "Vanderdecken's Message Home; or, the Tenacity of Natural Affection." The magazine printed it with no author's name. Some modern accounts credit John Howison, but the attribution is not firm, so the author stays unknown. The date and the text are firm. The story is the first full prose version of the legend, and the first the atlas has found that names its captain: Vanderdecken, of Amsterdam. The magazine gives him no first name.

Here is how the tale runs, in our own words. A ship leaves the Cape in a storm. The lookout, a sailor named Tom Willis, sees another vessel ahead, and the crew crowds to the bow. A chaplain, whom the sailors call Gentle George, listens as the old hands tell the legend. Long ago, they say, an Amsterdam captain named Vanderdecken fought a headwind off the Cape, cursing it, and swore he would beat about there until the day of judgment rather than put into the bay, whatever the devil might do. He is still out there, still trying.

Then comes the part that makes this version the most haunting. A boat from the phantom ship comes alongside with four men, and a stranger climbs aboard holding letters. He begs the crew to carry them to Europe. One is from a second mate to his uncle. One is for a sister, with a banknote inside. He speaks of a faithful wife left behind. He is polite and desperate.

The chaplain and the sailors tell him what he cannot bear to hear. The street where the uncle lived was pulled down sixty years ago. The bank that issued the note failed long since. The wife has been dead for many years. The stranger weeps and will not accept it. He keeps holding out the letters.

Nobody takes them. The stranger lays the letters on deck under a piece of iron and leaves, and the crew still will not touch them. The carpenter goes to fetch boards and nails to box them in, and before he is back a pitch of the ship tosses them overboard. The weather clears, and the sailors credit their escape from Vanderdecken. There are no dice in this story. There is no woman who can free him. The curse here is loneliness that has outlasted the people it was for.

Two small details matter. First, this is where the idea that accepting the letters brings misfortune enters the legend, and the lesson reads as refusal: do not carry what the dead cannot deliver. Second, the oath is about the Cape and about stubbornness. It is not about gambling, and it is not about love. Anyone who tells you this captain was a gambler is telling another version.

There is a naval footnote. In April 1823, HMS Leven, under Captain Owen, was off the South African coast when her crew saw what looked exactly like their consort Barracouta, about two miles to leeward, close enough to make out faces on deck. She was later found to have been some three hundred miles away. Owen printed the story in his 1833 narrative as an unexplained fact, not as proof of any ghost. In the same passage he repeats a sailors' tale that the Dutchman sometimes sends a boat with letters, and that a ship which takes them is doomed. That shows naval readers knew the letters motif by 1833. It does not show it is older than the magazine story.

The Blackwood's tale is also the likely home of a famous name. Many people think the captain was always called Van der Decken. In fact the name is missing from the 1790, 1795 and 1813 accounts, and the atlas finds it first here, in 1821, thirty-one years after the first known mention of the ship. Earlier sailors had a ghost with no name at all.
Hook: Another tradition gave the captain a different surname and put him in a different sea. In that one he plays at a table on deck, and his opponent is not a sailor.

#### ch-4 · A Captain With Many Names
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-4
sources: S4, S8, S2

Ask three people for the Dutchman's name and you may get three answers. Sailors, novelists and operas never agreed.

Start with the real man who is sometimes offered as a model. Bernard Fokke is described by later writers as a seventeenth-century sea captain from Friesland who worked for the Dutch East India Company, and he is remembered for speed. The retellings say that in 1678 he made the voyage from the Netherlands to Java in about three months and a few days, and handed letters to the governor there as proof of the timing. The tellers do not agree on whether the few days were four or ten, and the atlas found no original record of the passage, so the length of the voyage belongs to the told side. The same retellings say people whispered that he must have help from the devil, and that a statue of him on an island near Batavia was destroyed in 1808. Fokke is suggested as an inspiration by some writers. No source in the atlas shows that he was the origin of the legend, and nothing shows that he disappeared in a storm. Treat him as a rumour with a real face.

Next comes the German tradition of Herr von Falkenberg. The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica describes him as condemned to roam the North Sea on a ship that has neither helm nor steersman. In this version, the devil visits from time to time and plays dice with him on deck, and the stake is the captain's own soul. Here the setting has moved from the Cape to the North Sea, the captain is German rather than Dutch, and the curse has become a bet. The ship without a helm is an unusual image, and the atlas treats it as the identifying detail of the dice version, v-dice.

The same Britannica entry mentions a Dutch variant that gives the ghostly skipper a different name, and a Scott-style origin involving a plague and bullion. Compare all of these with the Blackwood's story from the last chapter. The Blackwood's captain takes an oath about the Cape. The Falkenberg captain takes a gambler's bet. One curse is stubbornness. The other is greed, or at least risk. A careful reader should not try to merge them.

This is also where the atlas teaches the main skill of the investigation. When a witness gives you a name, ask three questions. Which sea does the story put the ship in? What does the captain do in it: swear, gamble, wait or wander? And what do the sailors do when they see it? The answers sort the tellers into their versions faster than any name does.

There is a trap in that. Names are the easiest detail to borrow. A teller can say Falkenberg and still describe letters, or say Van der Decken and still describe dice. When that happens, the story has been mixed, and a mixed story is a weak witness.

A further point about the Dutch Golden Age. The age that built the fast ships, the insured cargoes and the long trade routes also produced the first stories about a captain who could not be insured against anything. A captain who gambles with the devil for his soul is the dark twin of a captain who gambles on a fast voyage for his fee. Fokke's reputation, as the tellers repeat it, shows how thin the line was.

By the 1830s, the legend had enough shapes that someone was bound to rearrange them. A German poet did. He took the ship, the captain and the curse, and asked a different question: what would it take to end it?

Hook: The poet's answer involved a calendar, a harbour, and a woman. The curse could be lifted, but only once every seven years could anyone try.

#### ch-5 · The Faithful Woman and the Stage
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-5
sources: S3, S1, S17

In 1834 the German writer Heinrich Heine published The Memoirs of Mister von Schnabelewopski, a satirical novel, in the first volume of his Der Salon, printed in Hamburg. Some reference works give 1833, but the volume itself carries 1834, so the atlas uses that year. Inside it, Heine retells the Flying Dutchman, and he changes the ending. The cursed captain may land once every seven years to look for a wife. If he finds a woman who stays faithful to him, he is free. In Heine's telling, his bride ends the curse by drowning herself. That is the version the atlas calls v-redeemer.

Pause on what this does to the legend. Until this moment, the ship had been a warning. Now it is a plot. Sailors who saw the ghost ship feared it. Readers of Heine's story were invited to feel sorry for it. The legend moved from the forecastle to the theatre, and the curse acquired a rule, a cycle and a way out.

Richard Wagner took that rule and built an opera on it. He wrote his first prose draft in Paris in May 1840 and finished the libretto by May 1841. The music was composed mostly in the summer of 1841, with the overture written last. He said that a stormy sea crossing from Riga to London in 1839, when he was fleeing his creditors, gave him the atmosphere. Der fliegende Holländer premiered on 2 January 1843 at the Royal Court Theatre in Dresden, with Wagner conducting.

In the opera, Senta, the daughter of a sea captain named Daland, vows to save the Dutchman through her faithfulness. When he believes she has broken that vow, he sails away. She follows him into the sea. Their deaths redeem him. Wagner first set the story in Scotland and changed it to Norway shortly before the Dresden premiere.

Notice what Wagner kept and what he dropped. He kept the seven years. He kept the faithful woman. He did not keep the letters. The messages to Amsterdam, the weighted bundle and the sailors who could only shake their heads have no place in the opera. Someone who knows the opera by heart does not know the Blackwood's story, and the reverse is also true.

The opera also shows how legends travel by changing their location. Wagner moved the story from Scotland to Norway because a stage needed a harbour, a spinning room and a quay. The Cape, where the legend began, is not the setting. The ghost ship that haunted a headland became a ship that visits a village.

For the investigation, this is the version that is easiest to confuse with the others, because it is the one most people know. If a witness mentions seven years, a woman or a vow of faithfulness, they are telling the Heine and Wagner story. They are not describing the sailors' ghost. The atlas will let you set them side by side in the table of versions and see where they part.

Be careful with one detail that people repeat. The faithful-woman rule is a nineteenth-century addition. It appears in none of the earliest printed mentions. Ask how the Dutchman's curse can be broken, and the answer depends entirely on which version you are reading. Under the oldest version, there is no cure at all.

Hook: Then, in 1881, a royal navy squadron's journal wrote down a sighting in plain words, with a time, a distance, and a number of witnesses. No woman, no dice and no letters. Just a red light, and thirteen people looking at it.

#### ch-6 · The Red Brig and the Mirror in the Air
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-6
sources: S5, S6, S10, S7, S2

The most famous modern sighting comes from a book first printed in 1886: The Cruise of Her Majesty's Ship "Bacchante", 1879 to 1882, credited to the two young princes who sailed in her, Albert Victor and George, with additions by their tutor. Their ship was part of a training squadron, and the passage in question lies between Melbourne and Sydney in July 1881. A detail that most retellings drop: for that leg the princes had moved to the flagship, HMS Inconstant, because the Bacchante was left behind in Melbourne for rudder repairs. So the sighting was not made from the Bacchante, even though the book carries her name.

The journal entry for 11 July says that at 4 a.m. a strange red light appeared, and in the middle of it the masts, spars and sails of a brig stood out sharply, about 200 yards away, coming up on the port bow. The lookout on the forecastle reported her close on the port bow. The officer of the watch, on the bridge, and the quarterdeck midshipman also saw her. The midshipman was sent forward at once and found no sign of any real ship, near or out to the horizon. The night was clear and the sea was calm. Thirteen people in all saw her, and the entry itself says that who she was must remain unknown. It adds that the two consorts sailing on the starboard bow flashed to ask whether the flagship had seen the strange red light. The journal also records that the man who first reported the vessel died in an accident later that morning. We mention it only briefly, and without detail, because it is a real person's death, and the atlas does not turn it into a spectacle.

Two cautions apply. First, the wording comes from the printed book, not the princes' manuscript journal or the ship's log, so it reaches us through the book's editors. Second, the entry is not signed by either prince, and it gives no position. The journal says the squadron passed Wilson's Promontory in Bass Strait on the night of 9 July and sighted Mount Diana to the north-east at 6.15 a.m. on the 11th, so the sighting lay somewhere off the south-east coast of Australia. Retellings that call it a Bass Strait sighting are stretching the text. The dates need care too. The book has the flagship mooring in Farm Cove, Sydney, at 9.15 a.m. on 14 July, which matches the Royal Collection Trust. The princes first went ashore on 15 July, and that is likely where the 14 versus 15 confusion comes from. Check every detail you hear against the printed text.

Now the explanations. Sailors on glassy nights are sometimes shown a distorted ship by the air itself. A Fata Morgana is a complex superior mirage that appears as a narrow band just above the horizon, stacking upright and inverted images and changing in seconds. It needs a strong temperature inversion, with warmer air over colder, denser air forming a duct that acts like a lens. Related is looming, where bent light lifts a distant ship above the horizon. The name Fata Morgana is Italian for Morgan le Fay, from the Arthurian stories, because such mirages were often reported in the Strait of Messina and described as fairy castles in the air. Some writers suggest that a mirage of a real ship lies behind the Dutchman legend. That is a proposal, not a proof. No source shows it as the origin.

The Bacchante night does not fit tidily. A mirage would not explain a ship that glowed red. A real vessel would have been found by daylight. The journal leaves the question open, and its own authors say so.

The legend also lives on in culture. It returns in the Wagner opera, in later novels, in children's cartoons and in theme-park rides. Our own tellings are new, and the atlas does not borrow the look of any film's ghost ship.

Hook: Which of these stories is documented, and which only told? The last chapter lays them side by side.

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: flying-dutchman--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S5, S9, S8, S7, S11, S16, S17

A good investigator keeps two lists. One is for things you can point to on a page. The other is for things people say.

The first list is short and solid. The Dutch East India Company founded a waystation at Table Bay in 1652. A travel book published in 1790 mentions sailors blaming the ship for stormy weather near the Cape. A book credited to George Barrington was printed in 1795, though modern scholars suspect it was not what it claimed to be. Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine printed the Vanderdecken story, unsigned, in May 1821. Heine published his retelling in the first volume of Der Salon, dated 1834. Wagner's opera opened in Dresden on 2 January 1843. A printed book from 1886 reproduces a journal entry dated 11 July 1881, kept aboard HMS Inconstant. Mirage science explains how air can fake a ship.

The second list is long and colourful. A captain who swore an oath that could not be undone. A gambler playing dice with a visitor on deck. A faithful woman and a seven-year cycle. Letters to people who died generations ago. A ship that is an omen of storms. A glowing hull with no lantern lit. None of these has a record outside the stories themselves.

The border between the lists is not always where you expect. Fokke is described as a real captain, and his fast voyage of 1678 is repeated in later writing, yet the atlas found no original record of how long it took, and the link between him and the ghost captain is only a suggestion. The Bacchante entry is documented as a printed text, yet what it describes is unexplained. The Barrington book is a real printed object, yet its contents may be invented. Always ask what exactly is documented: the event, the text, or only the telling.

There is also a third kind of statement, the confident detail that spreads because it sounds right. A famous example is that the captain was always called Van der Decken. In truth the atlas finds that name only from 1821, long after the first mention. Another is that Wagner's story is the legend. In truth it is one version, with an ending Wagner and Heine added.

How should a careful reader carry all this? Some details are documented and some are told. Before believing a detail, ask which version it comes from and whether any printed source stands behind it. When one witness contradicts another, do not guess. Go back to where each version first appears and see which details belong to which.

The legend survives because it is flexible. It is a ship, a captain and a curse, and each age fills in the reason. In the age of sail, it was a warning about the Cape. In the age of the stage, it was a story about loyalty. In the age of the log book, it was a puzzle with thirteen witnesses and no answer. That flexibility is what lets false stories sail under the same flag as true ones, and it is also what lets a patient reader tell them apart.

Hook: The door to the ship is open. Pick the version you believe, then find out what the room says.

#### Versions
anchor: flying-dutchman--versions

##### v-sailors
anchor: flying-dutchman--versions--v-sailors

The oldest and plainest account. A traveller's book from 1790 passes on what sailors said: that the stormy weather near the Cape was the work of a ship called the Flying Dutchman, which had not been able to find a pilot to guide it into harbour. In 1795 a second book, credited to George Barrington, added a Dutch warship lost near the Cape, and a later sighting, by sailors on another ship, of a vessel under full sail taken for her phantom. Neither mentions a captain by name or a curse of any kind. The tellers treat the ship as a condition of the sea, not a person. If a witness cannot give you a name, an oath or a reason, and only says the ship is seen before bad weather, you are hearing this version. Handle Barrington with care: modern scholars think the book may be a literary forgery. The sailors' version is also the version most easily outdone by later, richer stories.

##### v-letters
anchor: flying-dutchman--versions--v-letters

The 1821 Blackwood's story. Vanderdecken of Amsterdam is said to have sworn, long before the story's present, that he would beat about the Cape until judgment day rather than put into the bay, whatever the devil did. His ship is still sailing. A boat comes over from it, carrying a stranger with letters for people in Amsterdam: a second mate's uncle and a sister, with a faithful wife spoken of too. The listeners tell him the street is gone, the bank has failed and the wife is long dead. He weeps and keeps holding the letters out. In the end he leaves them under a piece of iron, the crew will not touch them, and the wind blows them overboard, and the weather improves. If a witness speaks of bundles, addresses, a refusal or a message that cannot be delivered, they are telling this version. In it, the curse has no gambling, no woman and no end.

##### v-dice
anchor: flying-dutchman--versions--v-dice

The German tradition of Herr von Falkenberg, as summarised in the 1911 Britannica. He is condemned to roam the North Sea, not the Cape, on a ship that has neither helm nor steersman. The devil comes to him from time to time and plays dice on deck for the captain's soul. This is a curse of wagering, and it has no messenger, no letters and no calendar. The image to remember is the ship without a helm. A witness who speaks of a table on deck, a cup and a visitor in the dark is telling this version. The same entry also lists a Dutch variant with another skipper and a Scott-style origin involving plague, so even inside this tradition the details are not fixed. The dice story and the letters story share a ghost ship and little else.

##### v-redeemer
anchor: flying-dutchman--versions--v-redeemer

Heine's 1834 retelling and Wagner's 1843 opera. The captain may come ashore every seven years, and if he finds a woman who remains faithful to him, he is released. In Heine's telling, the bride ends the curse by drowning herself, and the opera follows a similar path with Senta, daughter of the sea captain Daland. Wagner first set the story in Scotland and then moved it to Norway. This is the only version with a cure, a cycle and a love story. A witness who talks about a vow, a portrait or counting years on a calendar is telling this version. The letters of the Blackwood's story do not appear in it, and neither do the dice. It is the best known telling, and for that reason it is the one most likely to be mixed into the others.

##### v-log
anchor: flying-dutchman--versions--v-log

A published naval account, not a story. The 1886 book The Cruise of Her Majesty's Ship "Bacchante" prints a journal entry of 11 July 1881, written while the princes sailed in HMS Inconstant. At about 4 a.m. a red glow appeared and, within it, a brig with masts, spars and sails stood out about 200 yards away, crossing the bows. The lookout on the forecastle reported it near the port bow, the officer of the watch and a midshipman saw it, and thirteen people in all reported it. A midshipman who went forward found nothing, in a clear night and a calm sea. This version has no captain, no oath, no dice, no woman and no cure. It is the only account written by people who say they saw the ship, yet it names no culprit and gives no reason. We rely on the printed book and not on the manuscript journal or the ship's log.

## 7. Game world

### Setting
The player stands aboard a becalmed Dutch-built merchant ship at night, old oak weathered pale, sails hanging, a faint breath of fog on the water. The ship is not the film-style wreck. It is intact and orderly, as if the crew had only stepped away. A cold ember-red glow comes from somewhere below.

### Scenes

**sc-deck: The Becalmed Deck.** Left to right: a bare helm post with lashed lines and no wheel; a bundle of sealed letters in a wooden rack; a ship's bell on a bracket; a barrel head with a worn dice cup; a coil of rope under a brass spyglass rack; and at the far right a bank of fog on the water with a haze at the horizon. Mood: cold, quiet, watchful. Light: moon behind cloud and one ember-red lantern at the stern.

**sc-cabin: The Captain's Cabin.** Left to right: a stern window with leaded glass; a chart table with a map; a wall board with brass pins; an open logbook on a desk beside a stub of candle; a pewter goblet; a painted miniature of a woman on a bracket; and a sea chest at the right. Mood: private, close, sorrowful. Light: one candle and a faint moon through the window.

**sc-hold: The Lower Hold.** Left to right: stacked barrels with rope slings; a rusted chain pooled on the floor; a locker with flags; a hanging red lantern; and at the right a ladder leading to a hatch of moonlight. Mood: damp, echoing. Light: the red lantern, with moon from the hatch.

### Spirit encounter
The figure is the captain, seen as a tall shape in a long coat and a flat-crowned hat, standing in a doorway of fog on the deck. He never speaks in a made-up quote of a real person. He speaks in plain, short lines about his own condition, and what he says depends on the seed's version: an oath, a bet, a vow, a message or only a light. He asks three questions, one at a time, each tied to a puzzle slot. A wrong answer raises the fear meter by one step: the lantern dims, the cold deepens, the figure moves a pace closer. A right answer lowers the meter, the figure steps back and the ship's creak softens. If the fear meter fills, the scene resets to the deck without losing clues the player has already found.

### Breaking the curse
Final puzzle (pz-final, ritual-order): the player puts five story beats of the true version in the order the atlas tells them. For v-letters: the oath, the boat, the letters, the refusal, the clearing weather. For v-dice: the helmless ship, the visitor, the dice, the stake, the roaming. For v-redeemer: the arrival, the seven years, the woman, the vow, the release. For v-sailors: the refused pilot, the storm, the sighting. For v-log: the glow, the brig, the lookout's call, the midshipman's search, the empty sea. This is a story-ordering exercise, not an instruction of any kind. The relic earned is **The Harbour Lantern**, a brass lantern with horn panes whose small flame stays steady in any wind.

### Fear events
All are skippable, none block progress, and each has a reduced-motion form.
1. **fe-1 Bell once.** A single bell stroke from nowhere. Reduced motion: a text caption "a bell rings once".
2. **fe-2 Lantern bow.** The lantern light bows toward the stern and back. Reduced motion: a static dimming, no flicker.
3. **fe-3 Figure at the edge.** A silhouette stands in the far corner of the pan for two seconds. Reduced motion: it appears already visible and stays still.
4. **fe-4 Late reflection.** In the goblet or window, the reflection moves a beat late. Reduced motion: a still image with a caption.
5. **fe-5 Sails fill.** Slack sails swell without wind, then go slack. Reduced motion: caption only.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- **Film and game ships.** Do not echo any film, show or game ghost ship. Our ship is intact pale oak with cream canvas and an ember-red lantern: no skeletal crew, no green glow, no barnacles, no tentacles, no torn black sails, no named film props.
- **Real deaths.** The Bacchante entry mentions a sailor's death later that day. Atlas and game mention it in one clause at most, with no description, and never as a scare event.
- **Real people.** Prince George and Prince Albert Victor are named only as credited authors of a published book. No lines are put in their mouths. Heine, Wagner, Fokke and Scott are paraphrased, never quoted.
- **Barrington.** Always note that scholars suspect his book is a literary forgery.
- **Authorship of the 1821 story.** The magazine printed it unsigned. Some modern accounts credit John Howison; the atlas has not seen the evidence and says so.
- **Opera.** The atlas describes Wagner's plot in our own words; no libretto text or music is used. Ambient sound is original.
- **Religion and the occult.** The legend's oath and curse are told only as story beats. The atlas gives no instructions of any kind. The relic is a plain lantern.
- **Locations.** The map point shows a public headland as a place name only. The atlas does not encourage anyone to go to cliffs, coasts or the sea at night.
- **Theme-park rides and cartoons.** Mentioned generically; no names, logos or likenesses are used.
- **Translation.** Puzzle answers use plain nouns, dates and numbers, so they translate into Arabic without puns.
