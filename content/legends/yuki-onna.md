## 1. Cold open

The ferryman has gone home and left his boat on the far bank. Two woodcutters stand in the dark, snow driving sideways, and the only roof in sight is a hut the size of a cupboard. No fire. One door. The old man lies down and falls asleep at once. The young one stays awake, listening to the storm lean on the walls. Then the door opens, though nobody has touched it. White light, or white cloth, fills the doorway. Someone small and pale stands in the room, and the cold in the air is suddenly very still. She bends toward the sleeper. She breathes out, a thin white thread. And then she turns and looks at the boy who is only pretending to sleep.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · The Hut at the River
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-1
sources: S2, S1
Begin where Lafcadio Hearn begins, in the book Kwaidan, published in 1904. His story "Yuki-Onna" opens in a village in Musashi Province, in the countryside west of what was then called Yedo. Two woodcutters live there. One is an old man named Mosaku. The other is his apprentice, a lad of eighteen named Minokichi. Every day they walk about five miles to a forest, and the road crosses a wide river.

The river matters. In the tale, the crossing is made by a ferry, because floods keep carrying away any bridge that people build. So the two men depend on a boatman, and on the hour the boatman decides to stop. This is a small, true-feeling detail. A reader of the period would know such crossings: a flat boat on a rope or a pole, a landing on each bank, a hut for the ferryman near the water. Nothing grand, nothing magical, only a place where a day's work ends at the whim of weather.

One bitter evening they are caught by a snowstorm on the way home. When they reach the river, the ferryman has left and taken his boat to the far side. There is no choice but to wait. The men find the ferryman's hut, a tiny shelter of about two mats in floor size, with one door, no window and no fire. By the footnote in the English text, a mat room like this is roughly six feet square. Think of a cupboard with a roof.

The old man and the boy do the sensible thing. They close the door, put their straw rain cloaks over themselves, and lie down. The storm roars against the walls. Mosaku, tired from a long day, is asleep almost at once. Minokichi lies awake. He hears the wind. He hears the snow hitting the hut like thrown sand. After a while, even he begins to doze.

Something wakes him. A fine snow is blowing across his face, and the door, which he closed, stands open. A woman in white is in the room.

Keep that picture in mind, because everything in the legend grows from it. She is not a monster with fangs. She is not horned or hideous. The scare is the stillness: a person who is where nobody should be, and does not hurry.

Why a hut at a river, and not a mountain pass or a snowy field? A river crossing is an edge. It is the place between the village and the work, and between safety and the dark. Many of the oldest ghost stories happen at edges like this: bridges, fords, doorways, crossroads. The Snow Woman arrives where the human road runs out.

Notice also how plain the setting is. There are no shrines, no ruins, no cursed sites. The legend does not need them. A storm, a hut, two tired men, and a long night are enough. Winter itself does the work of a haunted house, and the cold does the frightening.

Hook: The woman bends over the old man. What does she do, and what does she say to the boy who is watching?

#### ch-2 · The Breath
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-2
sources: S2
[Dramatization] The snow stops blowing in the doorway. The room is as quiet as the inside of a bell. A line of pale breath drifts down from her lips and settles over the old man like a thin cloth, and he does not stir.

This is the heart of Hearn's tale, and it is told with great restraint. The woman, dressed in white, bends low over Mosaku and breathes on him. The text then moves on without describing what the breath does. The narration lets the reader understand, and then it looks away. This account keeps the same distance. What happens to the old man is told in a single line, gently, and not lingered on.

Then she turns to Minokichi. She leans so close that her face almost touches his, and she studies him for a while. Then she smiles, and she tells him what she had meant to do. She had intended to treat him exactly as she treated the old man. But she feels pity, because he is young, and a handsome boy. So she will let him live.

Mark the reason. She does not spare him from kindness in general, or because he prays, or because he is brave. She spares him because of his youth. The tale says so plainly, and that is one of the details that later versions and retellings sometimes change.

Then comes the condition. She tells him that if he ever speaks of this night, to anyone, even to his own mother, she will know, and she will come to him. The tale is firm about the reach of this promise: it covers every person, with the mother named as the closest one. Silence is the whole price. No gold, no oath on a sword, no ritual of any kind. Only a secret.

After saying this, she turns and goes out through the door. Minokichi springs up and closes it, and he looks for her in the snow, but she is gone. He turns to the old man. Mosaku does not answer, and the boy understands. The tale states the loss once, quietly, and moves on.

At dawn the ferryman arrives and finds Minokichi lying nearby, weak with cold and unable to speak. The boy is taken care of and slowly recovers. When he is well, he says nothing about the woman in white. He lives with the shock, but he keeps his word, and he goes back to the woods alone.

Look at what the tale has done. In a few paragraphs it has set up three things. A mysterious woman with a rule. A young man with a secret. And a long stretch of ordinary time in which the rule must be kept. Folklorists often note that promises of silence are a very old story engine, because every day the promise holds, the pressure to break it grows.

There is nothing to see in the hut now. The storm is over, and the white figure has gone. The boy is alive, and he is the only witness. Whether he really saw a woman, or only dreamed in the cold, is a question the tale leaves open, and Minokichi himself wonders about it later.

Hook: A year passes. On a road in winter, Minokichi meets a young woman he has never seen before.

#### ch-3 · The Girl on the Road
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-3
sources: S2
The next winter, Minokichi is walking home from work when he meets a tall, slender girl travelling the same way. She is beautiful, with a clear, pale skin. Her name is O-Yuki. Hearn's English footnote gives the meaning: Yuki is the word for snow, and the name reads like "Miss Snow".

She tells him her parents have died, and that she is on her way to Yedo, where she has some relatives who might help her find a place as a servant. Minokichi is taken with her. They walk on together, talking. By the time they reach his village they have grown fond of each other, and he invites her to rest at his home. She agrees, and she stays.

His mother likes her at once. She persuades the girl to remain a little longer, and then longer still, and the plan for Yedo is quietly dropped. In time O-Yuki becomes Minokichi's wife. The tale calls her an excellent daughter-in-law. When Minokichi's mother dies, about five years later, her last words are praise for the young woman.

The couple have ten children, boys and girls, all fair of skin. The villagers find this a wonder, and they find another one too: O-Yuki seems not to age. She looks as young as the day she came, and the neighbours remark that she is not like other women. The tale lets this detail sit without comment.

Then comes one night. The children are asleep. Snow is falling outside. O-Yuki is sewing by lamplight, and Minokichi, watching her face in the glow, says something he has never said. She looks just like a woman he saw when he was eighteen, he tells her, a white woman who came into the ferryman's hut. And then, breaking his promise without meaning to, he tells her everything: the hut, the breath, the old man. He adds that he has never been sure whether it was a dream or a real woman, a ghost perhaps, because she was so white.

O-Yuki puts down her sewing and stands over him. She says it was she. She reminds him of what she said, that he must never tell. She says she would kill him now, were it not for the children sleeping in the next room. She tells him to take good care of them, because if they ever have reason to complain of him, she will treat him as he deserves.

Then her voice grows thin, like the sound of wind. She turns into a white mist that rises to the roof beams and escapes through the smoke-hole. She is never seen again.

Notice three things about this ending. The children are the reason for mercy, which matches the first mercy: youth. She is never cruel on the page, only strict. And the home is left intact, a household with ten children and a missing mother. The story is sad, not violent.

It is worth saying plainly that the story does not call her evil or good. It does not explain whether she knew who he was on the road. It does not say whether her years in the village were a trick, a kindness, or a long, odd experiment in being human. The reader decides.

Hook: Where did Hearn hear this story? He says a farmer told it to him, in a place called Chofu. What is known about that claim?

#### ch-4 · A Farmer from Chofu
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-4
sources: S1, S2, S3
Lafcadio Hearn wrote many books for English readers about Japan. Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things appeared in 1904 from the publisher Houghton Mifflin, the year Hearn died. In his introduction, dated 20 January of that year, he says most of the stories were taken from old Japanese books.

One of them, he says, was not. The tale of the Snow Woman, as Hearn tells it, came to him from a farmer of Chofu, in the Nishitama district of Musashi Province, who gave it as a legend of his native village. Hearn adds two remarks. He does not know, he writes, whether the tale has ever been written down in Japanese. And the belief behind it, he says, once existed in most parts of Japan, in many curious forms. That is all the record says about the teller. The man has no name. His age is not recorded, nor how he came by the story, nor whether he told it in the same order and with the same details that appear in print.

That is an unusual position for a famous legend to be in. The most widely read telling of the Snow Woman rests on one unnamed speaker and one writer who passed it on. There is the farmer's claim, filtered through Hearn, and nothing earlier in the same form.

Some scholars have drawn a conclusion from this. One reading of the evidence, noted in commentary on the book, holds that Hearn's version may be the first written record of this particular tale, with its ferryman's hut, its promise, and its wife who leaves. Others point out that the origin of the story has not been firmly settled, and that Japanese folklorists collected closely similar tales only some decades after Hearn's death. Those two facts pull in different directions. A later collection might preserve an older oral story, or it might reflect the influence of Hearn's popular book. The record cannot tell us which.

So the Chofu claim needs careful handling. That Hearn said a farmer told him is documented, because it is in his own introduction. That the farmer's story was old, or that it was truly a village legend, is told, because there is no second witness.

The ferry crossing, the two-mat hut, the mother's death and the count of ten children come to us only in Hearn's printed telling. They may reflect what the farmer said, or they may reflect Hearn's own shaping. The record is silent on which. The printed tale is his.

There is also the matter of the name. In the tale, the wife is called O-Yuki, meaning snow. The footnote in the English text points this out directly. The reader is told the secret in the name long before Minokichi works it out. It is the kind of small, clever touch that suggests a storyteller shaping a tale, whether the farmer or the writer.

None of this reduces the tale. A story can be fine without a long pedigree. But the pedigree is thin. Behind Hearn's page are older Snow Women, with different habits and different endings, in places far from Musashi.

Hook: Long before Hearn, a printed book placed a white woman in a bamboo grove in the snow country of Echigo. Who is she?

#### ch-5 · Older Snow Women
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-5
sources: S4, S5, S3, S6, S8
Hearn's is not the only Snow Woman, and not the earliest. Reference works point to an Edo-period collection called Sogi Shokoku Monogatari, printed in 1685 and attributed to the renga poet Sogi, a figure of the Muromachi period who died in 1502. The attribution is not firm: the book was printed nearly two centuries after the poet's death, and the record is silent on how its tales reached the page. The tale in question places a white woman in a bamboo grove in Echigo, an old name for the Niigata region. She is described as very tall, about one jo (a traditional unit of roughly three metres), and called the spirit of snow. No hut, no promise, no wife. A figure, in a place, in winter.

Next comes a picture. In 1776 the artist Toriyama Sekien published Gazu Hyakki Yagyo, a three-volume book of drawn spirits, one to a page. The Snow Woman is among them, drawn as a woman in white among snow-laden bamboo. The book gives names and forms and, in most cases, few stories. So the picture shows us what an Edo-period audience could recognise, but not what story went with it.

Then come the regional tellings. Reference works record a number of them, and they are given here as reported. In Ojiya, in Niigata, a woman becomes a man's wife and later vanishes in the bath, leaving only icicles. In Kaminoyama, in Yamagata, a Snow Woman warms herself at an old couple's hearth, then turns into a whirl of snow and leaves by the chimney. In Tono, in Iwate, she takes children out to play in the fields on Little New Year. In Hirosaki, in Aomori, a warrior with a short sword tames one who is carrying a child, and is given treasure. In Oguni, in Yamagata, she is a princess from the moon who fell to earth and cannot return.

Look at the range. She lives in a bamboo grove, a hearth, a bath, a field. She is tall and fearsome, or domestic, or childlike, or a stranded princess. In some tellings she kills travellers, in some she spares them, and in some she marries and leaves. Reference works say Hearn's version has a sharper edge than many of the others; how much weight to give that is for scholars to argue.

What the tellings share is the season, the colour white, and the cold. Snow is a thing that arrives quietly and covers everything. A figure made of it is quiet too.

These tellings are not one tradition. A woman who warms herself at a hearth and a woman who steals up on sleepers in a hut share a name and a season, nothing more. The same name, used in many valleys, grew many stories. The careful reader asks of each one: where, and who tells it?

Hook: Hearn's hut, Sogi's grove, Ojiya's bath. Which Snow Woman is the real one, and where does the record place them?

#### ch-6 · The Snow Woman Today
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-6
sources: S3, S1
The Snow Woman has moved easily from the village to the page, the stage, the screen and the sketchbook. She appears in novels, plays, films, comics, animated series and games, in Japan and well beyond it.

What do retellings keep? Usually the white clothing, the pale skin, the long black hair against the snow, and the cold breath. Often the promise of silence stays too, because it is the part of Hearn's tale that makes a story out of an encounter. And sometimes the ending, with the woman melting into mist and leaving a household behind, survives in full.

What do they change? Many make her more dangerous, an outright killer who hunts travellers on mountain roads. Others make her tender, a lonely figure with a human heart. Some make her a warrior, a trickster, a mother. A few give her a fixed costume, a fixed power, a fixed name for her attacks. The older records do none of that. They give her a place and a season and, at most, one condition.

The older details are enough to picture her: a white kimono layered like drifted snow, hair held in a plain low knot, a straw hat dusted with frost, breath that shows as a thin line in lamplight. She carries no weapon. She does not shriek. She stands still, and that is the whole effect.

There is also a note on taste. The tale says that the old man does not wake, and then it moves on. The tellers keep that distance. They show the hut, the storm, the white figure in the doorway, the lamp, the mist at the smoke-hole. They do not show a frozen body or dwell on harm.

For readers who feel the story creep up on them in a quiet room: she is a character in folklore, studied by scholars and loved by storytellers. She is not a practice or a belief to follow. There is no instruction for calling or meeting her, because there is nothing to call. On a snowy night the safe thing is the sensible one: a warm room, a lamp, and someone to share it with.

There is one more reason she lasts, and it is not fear. It is that her story is about a promise, and everyone knows how heavy a kept secret can be. Minokichi does not break his word out of malice. He breaks it by accident, on an easy evening, in front of a warm lamp, with someone he loves. The scare is how ordinary the mistake is.

And so the legend reaches its last page. How much of this can be pointed to on paper, and how much lives only in the telling?

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: yuki-onna--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6
Every legend reaches a point where the storyteller must step back and let the record speak. This is that point.

Start with what is solid. Hearn's Kwaidan was published in 1904, and the tale "Yuki-Onna" in it can be read in full. It names the old woodcutter Mosaku and his apprentice Minokichi, aged eighteen. It places them in Musashi Province, with a ferry because floods wash out bridges, and a hut of about two mats. A woman in white breathes on the old man. She spares the boy for his youth and forbids him to speak of it, even to his mother. Next winter a girl named O-Yuki joins his household, becomes his wife, and bears ten children. One night he speaks, she reveals herself, and she leaves through the smoke-hole, never seen again. Hearn's introduction states that a farmer of Chofu in Musashi told him the tale.

Some things sit between the columns. The 1685 printing of the collection attributed to Sogi, with its white woman in an Echigo bamboo grove, comes from a reference site, not from the printed page. The same goes for Sekien's 1776 picture, and for the regional tellings from Ojiya, Kaminoyama, Tono, Hirosaki and Oguni. They are listed in reference works, but each one has not been traced to its original collection, so they stay reported.

Now what is told. That the farmer's story was an old village legend is told, since only Hearn reports it. That Hearn's is the first record of this tale is a scholar's opinion, and others doubt it. That the Snow Woman is the spirit of someone who died in the snow is an interpretation, not found in Hearn's text. That she stands for an older mountain goddess is another reading, with no firm record behind it. That the tale is a lesson about keeping secrets is a way of reading the story, not something it says.

The rule is simple. If a claim has no firm record that can be read, it is told, however often it is repeated.

One more note on method. When sources disagree, the account says so. The collection attributed to Sogi bears the name of a poet who died long before its printing. The year of Sekien's book is given as 1776 in some places and later in others. Hearn's own words about his source are one man's report of another man's words. Each version is given with its source.

Notice how much of the documented side is about people who wrote her down, painted her, or argued about her: a poet's name on a book, an artist, a writer, a farmer whose name was not kept. The record is a record of listeners. She herself signs nothing.

What survives is not one woman but a habit of telling: a storm, a small hut, a white figure at the door, a promise to keep, and a lamp-lit evening when someone says too much. The details move from valley to valley. The cold stays the same.

Hook: Outside, snow is still falling on a ferry landing, and a small hut waits with its door closed for the next traveller caught on the wrong bank.

**v-hearn**
anchor: yuki-onna--versions--v-hearn
In this telling the Snow Woman is quiet, courteous and strict. She spares a boy because he is young, she names one condition, and she returns as a good wife who lives with the family for years. The crisis comes at home, by lamplight, when the husband speaks. It is the only account with a ferry, a promise and a count of children.

**v-sogi**
anchor: yuki-onna--versions--v-sogi
In the collection attributed to Sogi, the figure is simple: a tall woman in white in a bamboo grove in Echigo, the spirit of snow. The attribution to the poet is uncertain, since the printing came long after his death, and the description comes through a reference site. A bamboo grove, great height and Echigo belong to this account. A ferry, a promise and a wife do not.

**v-ojiya**
anchor: yuki-onna--versions--v-ojiya
In the Ojiya tradition, as reference works give it, the Snow Woman marries a man of her own choice and later vanishes in the bath, leaving icicles behind. The reference does not describe any promise. Icicles, a bath and a wife who melts away belong to this account. It conflicts with Hearn on how she leaves and on why.

**v-picture**
anchor: yuki-onna--versions--v-picture
Sekien's page shows a woman in white among snowy bamboo and gives no story. It tells a reader what an Edo audience could picture, not what she did. Any claim that the picture shows her killing or marrying goes beyond the page, which carries no plot.

The legend is real as a story people tell. What matters is telling a documented line from a loved guess.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands at a snowbound river crossing at dusk, then inside a tiny ferryman's hut, then in a farmhouse at night with the snow still falling. A snow woman has not yet come, and every answer must be ready before she does.

**Scenes**

- `scene-landing` (the river crossing): From left to right, a bare willow with snow on its boughs, a pile of straw cloaks, the ferry boat drawn up on the bank, a wooden sign post without writing, the mouth of a flooded footbridge, a stack of cut logs under a woven mat, a stone lantern, the ferryman's hut at the far right. Mood: hushed, cold. Light: pale dusk glow low on the horizon, a single lantern at the hut door.
- `scene-hut` (the two-mat hut): From left to right, a pair of straw cloaks on pegs, a low wooden door, two sleeping mats, a small wooden bucket, a paper lamp on the floor, a woven straw hat, a smoke-hole in the roof showing sky. Mood: cramped, tense. Light: one paper lamp and cold blue light from the door crack.
- `scene-farmhouse` (the night at home): From left to right, a sliding paper door, a charcoal brazier with glowing coals, a sewing basket and a folded kimono, ten small futons in a row, a wooden shelf with a bath bucket, a sliding screen painted with snowy bamboo, a hearth with a hanging pot, the smoke-hole above. Mood: warm, then quiet. Light: the brazier glow and one lamp.

**Spirit encounter:** She arrives with the snow, not with a sound. A pale figure stands in the doorway, still. She never attacks. She asks one question at a time, in a low voice, about the night in the hut. A wrong answer makes the lamplight shrink and the fear meter rises with a long breath of wind. A right answer lets the snow settle, the room warms a little and the meter eases. At the end she asks the only question that matters: what was promised? The right reply, as Hearn tells it, is silence.

**Breaking the curse:** The final puzzle is her question. The player chooses what was promised from a row of cards. Silence is right in v-hearn. In other seeds the answer is the matching fact for that version (for example the bamboo grove in v-sogi, or icicles in v-ojiya). A right answer lets the snow stop and earns the relic: a small woven straw charm, a snow-dusted rain hat made for a child.

**Fear events** (never block progress; static fallback when reduced motion is on)
1. A line of breath-mist drifts across the lamp and the flame bends.
2. The door slides open a hand's width, then closes.
3. A distant scrape of snow sliding off a roof.
4. A pale shape stands in the corner and does not move.
5. The player's last tap leaves a faint frost mark that fades.

- Map point is the city of Ojiya, a public place. The legend has no single home. Do not suggest travel to any river, hut or mountain pass, and do not suggest going out in a snowstorm.
