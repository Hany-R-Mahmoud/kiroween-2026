## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] The village has gone quiet. A lantern hangs from one doorway and lights only the first step. Beyond the last mud-brick wall the sugarcane stands black and still, and past it a canal runs low and slow between reeds, a thin crescent of moon lying on the water. Two men are walking home. One of them stops. He has heard his own name, said gently, from far off, by someone who is not either of them. His friend has heard nothing. His friend says his name back to him, twice, and the man does not turn.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · The Voice That Knows Your Name
anchor: naddaha--ch-1
sources: S3, S4, S5, S14, S15
The belief is small and clear. At night, near the Nile or one of the canals that carry its water to the fields, a woman's voice calls a man by his own first name. He is the only one who hears it, or the only one who cannot resist it. Whoever answers and follows goes toward the water and is lost. That is the whole shape, and everything else told about her is a variation on it.

The name is Arabic: al-Naddaha, written النداهة. Reference summaries translate it as "the one who calls", or "the caller" (S3, S4, S5). Those are reference pages and not dictionary entries, so the meaning is reported and not checked. It fits what she does. She does not chase, appear or grab. She calls.

The reference pages place the belief in rural, farming Egypt, along the Nile and the canals (S3, S4). One of them says it was better known in the twentieth century, when more people lived and worked close to the water, and that it is less common in cities today though still familiar to young people (S3). Another says plainly that the origin in Egypt is unknown (S5). No dated record of a first telling was found in the sources read here. The belief is therefore best treated as living oral folklore: passed from person to person, changed in the passing, and not fixed to a single text, a single village or a single year.

Three details recur across the summaries. The first is the voice, which is described as soft and sleepy and strangely carrying, a voice that does not shout (S3, S5). The second is the name: she knows who the man is, and says so by using it. The third is the water. The canal, the riverbank and the reeds are where the story takes place, and the fields at night are the road to them.

What the sources do not give is a long narrative. No single plot is told in the same way everywhere, and the summaries read here do not follow a named person through a named event. This is common in village belief. Many such figures survive as a warning with a few fixed features, repeated to children and to young men who walk home late, and not as a tale with a beginning and an end.

The warning attached to her is the plainest part. Do not answer a voice at night that you cannot place, and above all not one that uses your name. A walker is better with a companion, because, in the most common telling, it is the companion who is able to break the spell.

It matters that her power is entirely in sound. She is rarely described close up. The summaries say she is only seen in rare sightings, far off by the riverbank, still, in a pale loose dress (S3, S4). Most tellings leave her unseen and let the voice do the work.

Arabic press reports add two details. One describes her as a beautiful woman or as an old one, and places her in fields and open ground as well as by the water, on dark nights or in the heat of midday (S14). The same report and a second one say that adults have used the story to frighten children, to bring them to obedience or to keep them from the water after dark (S14, S15). Both are newspaper pieces, so these details are reported and not checked.

That is why the story travels so easily. It needs no monster and no weapon. A person alone on a dark road, a quiet voice, and the pull of a name are enough.

The next question is what happens when there are two men on the path.

Hook: If only one man hears the voice, what does the other one do?

#### ch-2 · Two Men on the Path
anchor: naddaha--ch-2
sources: S3, S4, S5
The most repeated telling begins with two men. They are walking at night near the Nile or beside a canal, perhaps coming home from the fields or from a neighbour's house. The reference summaries say the figure usually appears to men who are in a pair (S3, S4).

She calls one of them by his first name. The summaries say he falls silent, becomes dazed and obedient, and begins to walk toward the voice (S3, S4). His companion has heard nothing, or at least is not bound by it. He sees his friend change. He speaks to him, takes hold of him, and tries to hold him back. In the telling, the effort works only slowly. The called man has to be shaken out of the spell, and the voice keeps going for as long as it takes. When he finally comes to himself, the two of them run, and the summaries say that the voice seems to echo behind them as they go (S3, S4).

The structure is worth noticing. Nobody in this account fights the Naddaha. Nobody argues with her, charms her or tricks her. The only action that counts is one person staying beside another. The remedy is company.

She herself is seen only in glimpses. The summaries describe a tall, slender woman with long hair, wearing a loose, nearly see-through dress, standing very close to the water and not moving (S3, S4). One article adds that the men usually never get close enough to see her clearly (S5). In the way the story is told, that distance is part of its force. She is a figure at the far side of the water, and the whole path between her and the man is dark.

Her voice is the clearest feature. A reference page calls it soft, sleepy and hypnotic (S3). Another calls it gentle and soothing yet strangely loud (S5). Those two phrases describe the same difficulty: a voice that sounds calm and kind, and yet reaches further than a calm voice should.

Notice what the story asks of the called man. He does not decide to leave in an ordinary sense. He is simply no longer able to hear anything else. That is why the companion matters so much. The only person who can speak into his silence is a person who is standing next to him.

Some summaries add a last note to this telling: that the companion may himself be at risk afterwards. One of them mentions a modern novel in which a man who stops another from reaching her is called next (S3). That is a literary addition, taken up again later, and not part of the plain village warning.

A fair question remains: what if there is no companion, because the man is at home? The next chapter turns to the version told for men who are indoors, and to the nights of waiting that come before the voice is obeyed.

Hook: What happens to a man who is called while he sits in his own house?

#### ch-3 · Nights of Waiting
anchor: naddaha--ch-3
sources: S3, S4, S5, S13
A second telling takes place indoors. In farming villages, houses sit close to the water, and the summaries say that the Naddaha can call men from inside their homes, not only from the path (S3, S4, S5).

In this telling the called man does not leave at once. The summaries say that he becomes distracted and uneasy for several nights, as if he were listening for something, and then leaves the house late at night (S3, S4). The delay changes the mood of the story. The first version is a moment on a dark road. The second is a slow worry in a household: a man who eats less, sits apart, looks toward the door, and is no longer quite with his family. The people around him do not know yet what he is hearing.

Both versions agree on what comes next. The called man is believed to be doomed, and a cure is often thought to be out of reach (S3, S5). Reference pages record this as the belief of rural people, and nothing in them shows a case that was checked. Many older people are said to believe that she leads her victims into the water and that is where they are lost, though one summary adds that no case is on record of a man actually seen to be harmed (S3). That one detail matters: the belief is much stronger than the evidence. The pages do not point to a named person, a date or a place, and the story stays at the level of what people say.

One writer who grew up in Cairo offers a reading of why such a belief might have lasted. She suggests it may have been fed by rural families watching their sons leave the fields for school or for work in the city, a loss no one could undo (S6). That is an interpretation by one author in a short course entry, not a finding of folklore research, and it is kept here as a reading and not as a fact.

The setting is important to this version. The details people remember are ordinary ones: crickets, unlit dirt roads, old trees, the river at night (S6). They are the sounds and shapes of a farming village after dark, and they give the story its feeling of being close at hand. Nothing in it needs a stranger or a faraway place.

The two tellings together make a small system. On the path, the remedy is a friend. In the house, the remedy is unclear, and the story admits it. The doom is announced and the waiting is described, but the way out is left unexplained. That is part of why the figure is feared. A danger that can be shaken off by a companion is manageable. A danger with no stated cure is a cause for lasting dread.

The record also shows what is not there. No archive, court file or village register was found naming a person said to have been taken by the Naddaha. The figure lives in speech, in one folklorist's survey of spirit beliefs (S13), and in print as fiction. The next chapter turns from what is told today to what a careful observer wrote down in the villages of Upper Egypt almost a century ago.

Hook: Did anyone who wrote about village beliefs a century ago record a voice that calls a person by name?

#### ch-4 · What a Visitor Wrote Down in 1927
anchor: naddaha--ch-4
sources: S1, S2, S12
Winifred Blackman's The Fellahin of Upper Egypt was published in London by Harrap in 1927. It describes the religious, social and industrial life of village farmers and looks for survivals from ancient times (S1). A scan of the first edition is on the Internet Archive (S1). The book is one of the few detailed written records of village belief from the period, and it is the most useful place to ask whether the Naddaha, or anything near her, appears in the early record.

In the parts read for this account, the name Naddaha does not appear. No figure who calls a man by name from the water is described. That is a finding about the passages checked and not a claim about every page of the book, and the record on this point stays open.

What the book does record is a village world in which the night and the water carried their own beliefs. In the first chapter, on villages, the author notes that peasants were afraid of the desert after dark, because of hyenas and because of beings the villagers call 'afarit (S1). A later chapter on these beings says that springs and wells in empty places were thought to belong to them (S1). It also records a belief about a canal called Bahr el-Yusuf: after a man was lost in its water, the villagers said that no woman would fill her water-jar at that spot (S1). The line is a single clause and nothing more is made of it here.

One passage is close in shape to the Naddaha, without being her. In the chapter on birth and childhood, Blackman writes of the karineh, a kind of double each person is believed to have. In one story, a poor widow's karineh called her by name three times while she slept, then led her down the street and put money in her hand (S1). The double here is a helper and not a danger, and it calls a sleeper, not a walker. It shows only that a voice using a person's name, at night, was a recognised idea in the villages of that region and period.

That is as far as the evidence goes. The calling voice and the water spirit are both in the written record, but separately. The two have not been found joined in a single figure in the parts of Blackman read here. Nothing in the book gives a reason to call the Naddaha ancient, or to call her new.

The other scholarly lead is Hasan El-Shamy's Folktales of Egypt, published by the University of Chicago Press in 1980 in the Folktales of the World series (S2). The catalogue record confirms the book and its date. It does not list a tale about the Naddaha, and the book's text was not available to read, so whether she appears in it is not known.

A third printed record is Ahmad Amin's Dictionary of Egyptian Customs, Traditions and Expressions, published in Cairo in 1953 by the press of the Committee for Authorship, Translation and Publication (S12). It is arranged alphabetically, and a search of its full text finds no entry for al-Naddaha. The text is a scan with uneven character recognition, so the absence is a finding about the text as read. What the dictionary does record is a bogey used to frighten crying children, named al-Ba'ba' and also Abu Rigl Maslukha, described as a creature that is human above and donkey below (S12). It belongs to the nursery and not to the canal, and the entries read give it no calling voice.

The records read for the years up to 1953 are therefore silent on her by name. The next chapter turns to the period in which she is certainly in print.

#### ch-5 · The Caller in Print and on Screen
anchor: naddaha--ch-5
sources: S3, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S11, S13, S14
By the second half of the twentieth century the figure was in Egyptian literature and film. The details come from reference works and listings, and they are given here as reported.

A prose work titled al-Naddaha by the Egyptian writer Yusuf Idris is dated 1969 in a French encyclopedia entry, which gives it the French title La Sirène (S7). The same entry places it alongside a work of 1971 and says that characters in both are broken and persecuted in their dealings with authority (S7). The English-language Wikipedia article on Idris lists al-Naddaha among his short stories under the English title "The summons" and gives no year (S9). Other accounts call it a novel. The form of the work is therefore reported inconsistently, and this account calls it only a prose work. Idris was born in 1927 and died in 1991 (S9). The work is under copyright. Its plot is not retold here; it is enough to say that it takes the title and the idea of a call that draws a person away.

A film called al-Naddaha, English title The Name Caller, is listed for 1975, directed by Hussein Kamal, with the actress Magda in a leading role, and based on a story by Yusuf Idris of the same name (S8, S11). The listing calls it a drama. Its story is not retold here. A short course entry by a doctoral student reads the film as using the myth as an allusion to the city, which draws villagers away (S6). That is one reader's interpretation, and the film's story and the folk belief are separate things. A 2014 newspaper report also places the film in 1975 with Magda in the lead (S14).

One scholarly voice sits outside literature and screen. In his study of Arab folklore and myth, available in a free edition from Hindawi, the Egyptian folklorist Shawqi Abdel Hakim lists al-Naddahat (النداهات) among beliefs about spirits that still circulate in Arab societies (S13). He proposes that al-Naddaha and a second night figure, al-Sal'uwa (السلعوة), descend from the Greek Lamia, a figure of myth who lures lone men, and he traces the wider family of such beliefs to peoples of the ancient Near East (S13). This is an interpretation. The study gives no chain of transmission from Greece or the ancient Near East to the Egyptian villages, and the sources read for the village belief do not mention one.

The Naddaha has also moved into popular horror. The novelist Ahmed Khaled Tawfik wrote a work called The Legend of El Naddaha, which two reference pages cite, and one of them records the idea in it that a man who stops another from reaching her would be called next (S3, S5). The Netflix series Paranormal features her, and the comedy series El Kabeer Awy plays her for laughs in one episode (S3). These are modern retellings and do not add to the village record.

In every modern use, the trait that survives is the call. Even where the setting is a city, the story keeps the idea of a voice that knows who you are and asks you to leave where you are.

It should not be assumed that the film or the novel preserve a village belief intact. Authors adapt a folk figure for their own purpose, and a writer may use the name to mean something the village never did. What can be said is that Egyptian writers and film-makers found the figure useful enough to name works after her, within a few decades of the period when the belief is said to have been strongest.

What remains is to sort what can be pointed to on a page from what is only told.

#### ch-6 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: naddaha--ch-6
sources: S1, S2, S3, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S12, S13, S14, S15
Some things about the Naddaha rest on a page. Winifred Blackman's The Fellahin of Upper Egypt was published in London in 1927, and the text read includes a double who calls a widow by name three times in her sleep, a village fear of the desert at night, and a canal spot where a woman would not fill her jar (S1). Hasan El-Shamy's Folktales of Egypt appeared in 1980 from the University of Chicago Press, according to a library record (S2). Ahmad Amin's dictionary of 1953 has no Naddaha entry in the text as read, and records a bogey used to frighten children (S12). Shawqi Abdel Hakim's folklore study names al-Naddahat among spirit beliefs that still circulate (S13).

Some things are reported by reference works and were not checked against an original. The meaning of the name, "the one who calls", and the descriptions of the figure and its variants are reported (S3, S4, S5). So are the dates of a work by Yusuf Idris, 1969, a film of 1975, and the credits listed for the film (S7, S8). The same holds for the Tawfik novel, the Netflix series and the comedy episode (S3, S5).

The told side is large, and it is the heart of the legend. That a voice calls men by their first names is told. That a man who is called is doomed, and that a cure is rarely possible, is told. That she leads men to the water and that this is where they are lost is told. That a companion can pull a man back is told. That a called man may spend several nights uneasy before leaving is told. That she may appear as an old woman, and that the story is told to keep children from the water at night, are told too (S14, S15). No dated case, named person or named place was found for any of it.

Some things sit in between. The origin of the belief is unknown, and a firm date cannot be given (S5). The idea that the legend grew out of families watching their sons leave the villages is one writer's reading (S6). The idea that the 1975 film uses her as a picture of the city is a reading too (S6). So is the idea that she descends from the Greek Lamia (S13). Both are kept on the told side, as interpretation.

The rule is simple. A claim that can be read on a page can be sourced. A claim that can be read only in the telling stays told, however often it is repeated. The figure of the Naddaha belongs to the telling, and the record holds only the signs that people have been telling it.

A method note: a modern reader may meet a popular claim about a yearly maiden given to the Nile, and may find it joined to her story. That is a separate story, and none of the sources read here records it as part of this belief. It is left out. Where a reference page and a primary text differ, the primary text was given the weight.

What lasts is the shape: a dark path, a lantern behind you, a voice that says your name from the water. The plain warning that goes with it asks only that one not answer it.

Hook: On a dark bank far across the water, something pale stands very still, and it has just said your name.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands at the edge of a mud-brick village at night, beside a lantern on a doorway, then walks the canal bank between reeds and a waterwheel, then reaches the open bank of the Nile under a crescent moon. Somewhere across the water a soft voice says a name.

**Scenes**

- `scene-edge` (the village edge): From left to right, a mud-brick wall with a shuttered window, a doorway with a hanging lantern, a clay water-jar by a step, a stack of sugarcane stalks, a pair of worn sandals, a bundle of maize leaves, a low field of maize going dark, a palm trunk, a wall marked with scratched tally lines. Mood: quiet, held breath. Light: one lantern at the door and a thin crescent moon.
- `scene-canal` (the irrigation canal): From left to right, a stand of tall reeds, a wooden waterwheel (sakia) with clay pots on its rim, a footbridge of palm trunks, a long straight canal with the moon on it, a shaduf with a counterweight, a coil of rope, a flat stone with a folded cloth, a row of palms leaning over the bank. Mood: slow, watchful. Light: the crescent moon on the water and a distant lamp.
- `scene-nile` (the open river bank): From left to right, a stretch of sugarcane as tall as a wall, a small wooden boat pulled up on the mud, a lantern left on the ground, the wide river with a far bank, a flat reed mat, a half-hidden pale shape among reeds far across, a path leading back, a fallen palm frond. Mood: wide, still. Light: the crescent moon and a faint glow from a distant town.

**Spirit encounter:** The Naddaha is never close and never shown with a face. She is a pale veiled shape far across the water or half hidden in the reeds, and mostly she is a voice. She says the player's chosen name once, softly, and then asks one question at a time in a low, even voice. A wrong answer dims the lantern, lengthens the distance between player and companion marker, and raises the fear meter with a longer, softer call. A right answer lets the call fade and the shape take a step back into the reeds. At the end she asks which telling the player believes by naming a single detail that belongs to it.

**Breaking the curse:** The final puzzle is her last question. The player chooses, from a row of small cloth tokens, the detail that matches the true account: in v-path the companion who pulls him back; in v-home the nights of waiting; in v-blackman the double that called a sleeper by name; in v-modern the film of 1975. A right answer lets the voice go quiet and the pale shape turn away from the water. The relic earned is a small tin lantern with a pierced pattern, lit and held low.

**Fear events** (never block progress; static fallback when reduced motion is on)
1. A soft far-off call is heard once, with no words.
2. The lantern flame leans toward the water and returns.
3. The reeds sway on the far bank though the air is still.
4. A pale shape appears at the edge of the field, then is gone.
5. The moon's reflection on the water breaks into rings and settles.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Village folklore, not religion or practice. No scripture, doctrine, mosque, saint or exorcism drives any chapter. Where Blackman uses the word 'afarit for village spirits, it is reported as a village word and not explained.
- Loss is stated in one neutral clause at most. No water-death scene, no body and no harm is shown in art. The canal-spot report is given as one clause.
- No living private person is named. No village is named as haunted. The pin is the public city of Minya, chosen only as a public place on the river. Do not suggest visiting a canal, a field or any riverbank at night.
- The Naddaha is an original design: a far-off pale veiled silhouette, mostly a voice, never a face and never close. Do not copy the 1975 film al-Naddaha, any television version (including Paranormal and El Kabeer Awy), any comic, novel cover or game depiction. No revealing dress, no seductive pose, no fangs or claws.
- Yusuf Idris's al-Naddaha, the 1975 film and Ahmed Khaled Tawfik's novel are under copyright. They are named and described in a sentence or two, not retold and not quoted.
- Keep cultural detail respectful for Egyptian readers: no exoticising language, no "mystical Orient" framing. The popular claim of a yearly maiden given to the Nile is a separate story that none of the sources read records; it is not part of this file.
- Items not checked against a primary or scholarly text (tagged [R], never used in true-or-told slots): the meaning of the name, the descriptions in the reference summaries, the 1969 date, the film's year and credits, Idris's birth year, the Tawfik novel, the Netflix series and the map coordinates. The genre of Idris's al-Naddaha (story or novel) is reported inconsistently.
- S14 also quotes elderly women accused of being the Naddaha and a harm done to them. That material concerns real private people and is not used.
- S13 sits in a chapter on spirit beliefs with religious material; only the Naddaha and Lamia passage is used, and the Greek link is kept on the told side as interpretation.
- Open: whether Folktales of Egypt (El-Shamy, 1980) or Blackman's unread character range holds a Naddaha tale; the first-printing year of S13.
