## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] The fire in the long hall has burned down to a red line. Outside, the snow is blue under a high moon, and the roof of the house is covered with sod and frost. Everyone inside is awake and no one is speaking. Something heavy has stepped onto the turf above the ceiling. A beam creaks. Dust falls through the smoke-hole in a thin pale thread. The farmer counts the people who are still under his roof and gets a smaller number than last month. The step moves slowly along the ridge, stops over the door, and waits.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · The Word and the Sagas
anchor: draugr--ch-1
sources: S1, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8
A farmhouse in the Icelandic sagas is a plain thing: a long low building of turf and stone, a hall with a fire down the middle, a door that faces the weather. The stories told here all begin in such places. They begin when somebody who should be in the ground is not.

The word for that somebody is draugr. In Old Norse it was an old word with more than one sense. A reference page traces it to a very early root meaning something like a delusion or a deceiving appearance, and adds that the same word could name a dry tree trunk and, in poetry, a warrior (S5). Those details are reported from a reference work and not checked in a dictionary. What matters for the sagas is the use: a dead man who is back among the living, strong and cold, and a danger to a household.

Two other terms sit beside it. Aptrganga is glossed as an again-walker, a revenant, the one who walks again after death. Haugbúi means mound-dweller (S5). A common modern account says the mound-dweller stays near its grave and troubles those who intrude, while the draugr leaves the grave and troubles the living. That tidy line is a convenience of later scholars. The same reference page records that Nora K. Chadwick, in "Norse Ghosts: A Study in the Draugr and the Haugbúi", Folklore 57, no. 2 (1946), treated the two as one group (S5, S6). The article itself is cited here from a bibliographic listing and has not been read in full.

The debate goes further. Ármann Jakobsson argued in a 2011 article that two usual ways of studying the Icelandic undead are flawed. One leans on nineteenth-century definitions made by folktale collectors. The other counts every use of the word draugr in medieval texts and studies the vocabulary. He proposed sorting the creatures by what they do instead (S7). A reference page adds that the sagas often do not use the word draugr at all, preferring aptrganga for a revenant, and that Glámr, the best-known case, is called a troll in his own saga and not a draugr (S5). The label, then, is partly a modern one. This account uses it as scholars do, for a dead man who walks.

Three sagas carry the scenes told here. Grettis saga gives the shepherd Glámr and a night in a hall. Eyrbyggja saga gives Þórólfr Halt-foot, a farm haunted by a dead man, a second farm haunted by many visitors, and a legal ending. Laxdæla saga gives Hrappr and a doorway. All three are written texts of the thirteenth century or later, set in the settlement age and the early years of the Icelandic Commonwealth. A reference page dates Eyrbyggja saga to the mid or late thirteenth century and warns that, like the other family sagas, it rests on oral family history and not on a firm record (S8). The saga texts were read here in English on the Icelandic Saga Database (S1, S3, S4). Grettis saga also exists in the 1869 Magnússon and Morris translation, whose table of contents was checked (S2).

The sagas treat a walking dead man much as they treat a feud. There is a cause, a household, a toll counted in names, and a way to end it. None of them is written as an instruction. They describe a dead man who is heavy, strong and cold, and a farm that must be put right by neighbours, kin or a court.

The best place to begin is a winter valley, a rich farmer who cannot keep a shepherd, and the stranger the lawman sent.

Hook: Why does a farmer who cannot keep a shepherd take the one man the lawman sends him?

#### ch-2 · The Shepherd in Shady-vale
anchor: draugr--ch-2
sources: S1, S2
In Grettis saga the trouble starts with a farm and a shortage of hands. The farm is Þórhallsstaðir, "Thorhall-stead" in the English text, in a valley the translation calls Shady-vale; the Icelandic name is Forsæludalur (S1). The farmer, Thorhall, is rich in sheep and cannot keep a shepherd, because the place is haunted. Men take the work and leave. Some do not leave at all.

Thorhall asks for advice from Skapti the Lawman, a man of weight in the saga. Skapti sends him a worker named Glámr. He is a Swede, large, with wolf-grey hair and a harsh manner. The saga says he was disliked by most people. He is a strong worker and not a pleasant one (S1).

Then comes midwinter. On Yule eve, the evening before the midwinter feast, Glámr refuses the household's ways, goes out into a snowstorm and does not come back. The household finds him dead in the snow, with the ground around him showing signs of a hard struggle. What he fought, the saga does not show. The men try to carry him and cannot; the body is too heavy to move (S1). The weight is the first sign. A man who was strong in life is far heavier in death.

The haunting begins soon after. The household sees Glámr walking at night. He rides the roofs of the farm. People are afraid, and most of the household leaves (S1). What had been a farm with a shepherd problem becomes a farm that fewer and fewer will enter.

Thorhall hires a second shepherd, Thorgaut. Thorgaut laughs at the idea of Glámr's ghost, and at midwinter he is found dead. The old cowherd is killed in the cattle barn. Thorhall's daughter falls sick from the haunting and dies (S1). The saga counts the cost in people, one after another, in the way it would count the dead of a feud: a shepherd, a cowherd, a daughter.

Note how the saga builds the figure. Glámr does not plead or warn. He asks for nothing. He walks, rides the roofs, and the losses go on. The only question the saga asks is who can stop him.

The saga gives no rite as the answer. Its answer is a man. In the next chapter, a young man of large reputation hears the story and decides that it deserves a visit. His kinsman Jókull at Tongue tries to talk him out of it (S1). The kinsman sees what the young man does not: a place that has beaten several men can beat one more.

The tale also shows how the sagas handle a haunted farm without any village around it. There is a landowner, a lawman's advice, a hired hand, and a toll. The setting is practical and hard: heavy snow, short days, a long hall with a low fire. The valley has the cold of a real Icelandic winter, which is part of why the story still works when it is told in a warm room.

The Magnússon and Morris edition lists the chapters of this episode by the farm, the shepherd's doings, and the hero's arrival (S2). The saga's shape is that of a visit paid to a danger by someone who thinks he can bear it.

Hook: Who is the young man, and why does a warning from his own kinsman only make him go?

#### ch-3 · The Wrestling in the Hall
anchor: draugr--ch-3
sources: S1, S2
The young man is Grettir, the hero of the saga. He hears the story of Þórhallsstaðir from his kinsman Jókull and decides to go to the farm anyway. Jókull warns him that the danger is real, and Grettir answers by riding to the valley (S1).

He stays as a guest. On the first night nothing happens in the hall, but in the morning Grettir finds that Glámr has killed his horse. The saga treats this as a message. The ghost has been there, and the guest has been noticed.

On the second night Grettir waits, awake, in the hall (S1). Glámr comes. First there is the sound on the roof, the heavy tread along the ridge, and the creak of the beams. Then Glámr comes in. Grettir and Glámr seize each other and struggle. The saga describes the fight in terms of force and furniture: the two of them heave across the room, the benches go over, and the hall itself is wrenched apart by the struggle (S1). Neither man is described cutting or wounding the other. It is a wrestling match between a living man and a dead one, in a wooden hall with a fire dying in the middle.

The fight moves to the door. Grettir drives Glámr outward and the two of them go through the doorway together. They fall outside, and Glámr falls under the moonlight (S1).

That is the centre of the episode, and it is quiet. A ghost that has terrified a valley is brought down in the open air, under the moon, in the snow. The saga allows Glámr to speak at that moment. He tells Grettir that his strength will not grow any further, that he will become an outlaw, and that Glámr's eyes will stay with him. The words say also that Grettir will dread being left alone (S1). The speech is a prophecy and a curse at once, and it is told as the saga's own claim, not as a fact the narrator vouches for.

Then the saga ends the haunting in one plain clause: the body was burned and the ashes buried away from pasture and path (S1).

The curse does not end with the body. Grettir is, from then on, afraid of the dark. The saga says he cannot bear to be alone at night and sees things in the dark (S1, S2). The English text records a saying drawn from this: a person who sees terrors in the dark is said to have "Glamsight" (S1). Whether Grettir's fear is the ghost's doing or only the memory of a hard night is left open by the way the story is told. The saga lets the reader choose.

This is why the Glámr story has lasted. It has a heavy dead man, a roof, a door, a moon and a hero who wins but pays. It has no sword-play to describe and no mystery about who is to blame. The cost is a feeling: a man who once feared nothing now fears the dark.

The next story is of an older kind of ghost, one with a family, a farm and a long argument over where to bury him.

Hook: What do you do with a dead man who is too heavy to carry and will not stay where he was put?

#### ch-4 · The Man Who Was Moved Twice
anchor: draugr--ch-4
sources: S3, S4
Eyrbyggja saga begins its ghost with a quarrel between a father and a son. The father is Þórólfr Halt-foot, "Thorolf Lame-foot" in older English, a man who has grown harsh and unjust in old age. His son is Arnkel. After a failed meeting with Arnkel over a wood at Crowness, Thorolf goes home to Hvamm, sits down in his high seat, refuses food and is found dead there the next morning (S3).

Arnkel prepares the body himself. The hall door is too narrow to carry the dead man out, so Arnkel has a wall broken open. Oxen draw the body on a sledge to Thorswater-dale, where it is buried in a howe (S3).

Then Thorolf walks. Cattle yoked to the sledge go mad. Animals near the mound die. The herdsman of Hvamm is found dead near the howe, and later the goodwife of the farm dies of the troubles. The walking spreads across the dale and drives people away. Arnkel gives shelter to those who stay with him, and Thorolf does not reach them (S3).

In spring Arnkel asks the sons of Thorbrand for help. They open the howe, and the body has not decayed. They try to carry it to Vadils-head, but the oxen go mad and die, and the body proves too heavy to carry further. It is buried again on a nearby headland, which from then on is called Halt-foot's Head. Arnkel builds a high wall across the headland, so high that only birds can cross it. Thorolf stays quiet as long as Arnkel lives (S3).

Here is the second pattern the sagas use: not one fight, but a long property question. Where should the dead be buried, and how far? Each move is a practical solution that works for a time. The sagas treat a walker as a problem of land and labour.

After Arnkel's death, the trouble returns. Thorolf walks again at Lairstead and later at Ulfar's-fell, killing men and livestock. Thorod Thorbrandson gathers house-carles and neighbours, raises the body from the headland and ends the matter: the body was burned and the ashes were cast into the sea (S3). The saga shows that the end comes only after the person who had held the walker in check was gone.

Laxdæla saga has a shorter version of the same problem. Hrappr, a large and combative man who fled from the west and settled in Salmon-river-Dale across from Hoskuldstead, bullies his neighbours until they appeal to Hoskuld (S4). When Hrappr is old and bedridden, he asks his wife, Vigdis, to bury him standing in the doorway of his fire hall, so that he can keep watch over his household. She does as he asked (S4).

Hrappr's ghost then walks. It kills servants and terrorises the district until Hrappstead is empty. Vigdis goes to her brother Thorstein Swart. Hoskuld has the body dug up and moved to a remote place where people and cattle seldom pass, and the haunting eases (S4).

It returns in a later generation. When Olaf buys the land at Hrappstead from Trefill for three marks of silver and builds the new farm Herdholt, a house-carle meets Hrappr at the door of the fold. Olaf strikes at the figure with his spear. Hrappr wrenches it aside, snaps the shaft, and vanishes. The next day Olaf has the grave opened, and the body was burned and the ashes were scattered at sea (S4).

Both stories turn on a door. Thorolf's wall was broken to get him out; Hrappr asked to be buried in his own doorway. In Hrappr's case the saga links the odd burial to the walking that follows, and that link is the saga's own, not a rule it states.

Hook: At a farm on the same peninsula, the dead come in a group, and the walkers are not ended by a wall or a fire but by a hearing.

#### ch-5 · The Court at the Door
anchor: draugr--ch-5
sources: S3, S8
Eyrbyggja saga puts its best-known haunting at Fróðá, "Frodis-water" in the English text, on the Snæfellsnes peninsula (S3, S8). The goodwife is Thurid and the goodman is Thorod Scat-catcher. Their son Kiartan is the most important young member of the household. A large, rich woman named Thorgunna has come from the South Isles on a Dublin ship and lives there, keeping to herself. Thorir Wooden-leg and his wife Thorgrima are also lodged at the farm (S3).

The wonders come in a run. A dark cloud passes over, and its rain falls only on the hay of Fróðá. Thorgunna falls ill and dies, leaving instructions about her bed-gear. Thurid persuades Thorod to carry out only part of them (S3). The wonders follow in the next lines of the saga.

For a week, a half-moon is seen circling the hall each evening at the time of the fires. Thorir Wooden-leg names it the Moon of Weird and says that it foretells deaths (S3). The shepherd becomes withdrawn and dies. Then he is seen with Thorir. Thorir himself dies after a confrontation outside the door, and six more die (S3).

Strange things happen in the household. A heap of stock-fish is torn apart with no living thing found. A seal's head rises through the floor of the fire hall, toward Thorgunna's bed-gear, and Kiartan beats it down with a sledgehammer until it sinks through the floor (S3).

Then Thorod and his crew are drowned off Enni, and their bodies are never found. At the funeral feast Thorod and his men walk into the hall dripping wet and sit by the fire, and they come back each evening. Thorir and his followers appear too. A great tail rises from the stock-fish heap and slips out of the hands that try to hold it. Thorgrima dies and is seen with Thorir. By harvest, of thirty servants, eighteen are dead, five have fled, and seven remain (S3).

Kiartan asks his uncle Snorri for advice. Snorri sends his son Thord Kausi and six men to Fróðá. They burn Thorgunna's bed-gear and summon all the walkers to a door-court, a hearing held at the door of the hall (S3).

The procedure is the part to notice. Those who had sat by the fires are summoned from the nearby farms. Kiartan brings charges against Thorir Wooden-leg, and Thord Kausi brings charges against Thorod, for entering the household without leave and for taking men's life and luck. The case is heard in the manner of the assembly called the Thing: testimony is given, verdicts are read, sentence is passed (S3). As each sentence falls, the one it names gets up and leaves, unwillingly. When the last has gone, the walking is over, and Thurid recovers from her sickness (S3).

The saga turns a haunting into a lawsuit. The dead are treated as trespassers with a legal duty to leave, and the household as the injured party. The ending is not a fight and not a charm. It is a hearing at a door, with a summons, a plea and a judgment, the way an Icelandic farm settled a dispute with a neighbour.

The saga does not explain why the court works. It says only that it did.

Hook: Is the draugr a ghost, a monster, a legal problem, or only a word the later scholars gave to all three?

#### ch-6 · The Draugr Today
anchor: draugr--ch-6
sources: S1, S5, S8
The sagas gave the figure a fixed shape: heavy, strong, cold, at its worst at midwinter, and tied to a farm. Later readers have made of it something else.

A reference page points out that the collection of Icelandic folk tales made by Jón Árnason in the nineteenth century gave the name draugasögur, draugr stories, to a group of tales, and that Ármann Jakobson thinks that the modern category may not match what the word meant in medieval texts (S5). In other words, the idea of a standard draugr was partly built after the sagas, by those who collected and sorted them.

Modern popular culture has built its own draugr again. Popular versions often turn the creature into an armoured warrior or a guardian of a tomb. That image is not found in the saga passages read here. In the saga texts read here the dead man is not a guardian and not an enemy that can be chosen for a fight. He is a former neighbour or hired hand who has become a burden on a farm. The sagas give almost no description of his face. They give weight, cold, a tread on a roof, and eyes that catch the moonlight (S1).

One modern work is known from a reference page: a 1938 play called Fróðá, whose plot draws on Eyrbyggja saga (S8). The record read here does not describe it further.

What does the modern reader gain from going back to the sagas? A different kind of fear. A tomb draugr is met by a visitor who chose to go. A saga draugr comes to a house where people live and work, and no one chose. The dread is domestic. It is about the farm, the roof, the shared hall, and the sheep that still have to be fed in the morning.

A second gain is the sense of cost. Every walker in the sagas leaves a list of names behind: a shepherd, a cowherd, a daughter, a goodwife, a crew of drowned men. The stories are about what a community loses when it is not sure that its dead have gone.

A third gain is how they end. None of the endings is magic. A strong man wrestles in a hall; neighbours move a body and build a wall; a lord has a grave opened; a court sits at a door. The sagas make the ghost a problem for people to solve together, with muscle, patience and a hearing. That may be the reason they keep being retold.

Strip the retellings away and the saga figure is plain: a huge dark shape against the moon, heavy and still, with eyes that catch the light.

The last chapter sorts what can be pointed to on a page from what is only told.

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: draugr--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8
The record can be read in three places. In Grettis saga, as read on the Icelandic Saga Database, a farmer named Thorhall at Þórhallsstaðir in Shady-vale takes a Swede named Glámr as shepherd on the advice of Skapti the Lawman. Glámr dies in a snowstorm on Yule eve, is too heavy to move, and walks. Thorgaut, the old cowherd and Thorhall's daughter are lost to him. Grettir fights him in the hall, they fall outside under the moon, and the saga gives Glámr words about Grettir's fear of the dark. The body was burned and the ashes buried (S1, S2).

In Eyrbyggja saga, Þórólfr Halt-foot dies in his high seat, is buried in Thorswater-dale, walks, is moved to Halt-foot's Head behind a high wall, and walks again after his son Arnkel's death, until Thorod Thorbrandson ends it. At Fróðá, a series of wonders ends with a door-court at which Kiartan and Thord Kausi bring charges (S3). In Laxdæla saga, Hrappr is buried standing in the doorway of his fire hall, walks, is moved by Hoskuld, and is ended by Olaf at Herdholt (S4).

Some things sit between the columns. The meanings of draugr, aptrganga and haugbúi, the Proto-Germanic root of the word, the dating of Eyrbyggja saga, Chadwick's 1946 article and Ármann Jakobsson's 2011 article are reported from reference pages and abstracts, not from the original articles read in full (S5, S6, S7, S8).

The told side is large. That the sagas record real events at real farms is told: they are written texts of a later age, and the only account of the Fróðá deaths read here is the saga itself. That the door-court ended the haunting is the saga's own claim. That Glámr's words caused Grettir's fear of the dark is the saga's own claim as well. That Hrappr walked because of how he was buried is the saga's link, and a modern reader may reject it. That the draugr and the haugbúi were two clearly separate figures in every writer's mind is a modern scholarly convenience, and the debate over it is not settled (S5, S7).

The rule is the same as for any story kept on paper. If a claim can be read on a page, it can be sourced. If it can be read only in the telling, it stays on the told side, however often it is repeated.

The method note is short. Where the saga text and a reference page differ, the saga text was given the weight. The chapter numbers in this file follow the Icelandic Saga Database and may differ in other editions.

What survives is a shape: a farm in winter, a heavy step on the roof, a name counted among the lost, and, at the end, a way for living people to make the dead leave. The details change from saga to saga. The shape does not.

Hook: Outside a turf house, a very large shape stands against the moon and does not move, and it is looking at the door.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands in a snow-covered farmyard at night beside turf houses, then inside a long hall with a low fire, then on a path above a moonlit fjord. A heavy step is somewhere above the player, on a roof.

**Scenes**

- `scene-hall` (the long hall at night): From left to right, the high seat with a worn fur, a doorway with a narrow frame, a low central fire in a stone trough, a bench knocked askew, a wooden bowl of dried fish, a small bundle of folded bed-linen, a hammer on a peg, a smoke-hole with a pale thread of dust, a bench with a wool blanket. ## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Folklore and literature, not religion or practice. No priest, saint, holy water or church rite drives any chapter. The Fróðá ending is told only through the door-court, a legal hearing. Details of the saga text that involve a religious setting or a priest's actions are left out on purpose.
- Fights have no gore. Endings by burning are given as one plain clause ("the body was burned and the ashes buried") with no physical detail. No beheading or wound is described.
- No grave or mound is a destination. The map pin is the public town of Ólafsvík. Do not suggest visiting a burial site, a mound, a headland wall or any private farm.
- The draugr here is an original design: a huge dark shape against the moon, heavy and still, with eyes that catch the light. Do not copy the Skyrim draugr, any Marvel draugr, any God of War draugr, or any other film, TV, comic or game version. No armor, horns or skull faces.
- The saga texts are medieval and paraphrased only; no quotation exceeds a few words. The translation credit of the Icelandic Saga Database texts was not shown in the fetched pages, so their public-domain status is not confirmed.
- Items not checked against a primary or scholarly text (tagged [R], never used in true-or-told slots): the etymology of draugr, the glosses of aptrganga and haugbúi, Chadwick's 1946 article contents and date, Jakobsson's 2011 date, the dating of Eyrbyggja saga and the 1938 play.
- Chapter numbering follows the Icelandic Saga Database and may differ in other editions (one reference page places Thorolf's death in a different chapter).
