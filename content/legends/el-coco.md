## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] The candle has burned down to a thumb of wax. In the cradle the baby sleeps, calm, one fist open on the blanket. On the whitewashed wall a shadow has been growing for a while: a hood, a pair of shoulders, no face. It is only the coat on its peg, caught by the flame. The mother knows this and hums anyway, the old tune with the old threat folded into it: sleep, or the Coco comes. She has hummed it a hundred nights. Who taught her the song? Who taught them? Outside the shutters the street is quiet, and on the sill a brown nut with three dark marks looks back at the candle like a small round face.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · A Word Without a Face
anchor: el-coco--ch-1
sources: S1, S2, S5, S6
The Coco has no face. Every source read for this account agrees on that by saying nothing about it. Spanish and Portuguese parents have used the name for centuries, and not one of the texts describes eyes, a mouth or a nose for the creature. It is a hooded shape, a dark lump in a corner, a name said to a child at bedtime.

The oldest dictionary read here that holds the word is the Tesoro de la lengua castellana o española of Sebastián de Covarrubias, printed in Madrid in 1611 (S1). It gives coco two separate entries. In the first, coco is the fruit, the nut of the Indies, and the entry spends most of its length on the husk, the hard inner shell and its three holes that look like eyes and a mouth. The second entry is one short idea: in the speech of children, coco means a figure that causes fright. One point stands out. By 1611 a Spanish dictionary-maker treated the nursery sense as ordinary, and as the children's own way of talking.

A nearby entry, cocar, shows a third strand. To cocar, or to "hacer cocos", is to make faces and noises in order to scare children, and Covarrubias says the verb copies the sound that a monkey makes to frighten the boys so that they will not harm her (S1). Already the word is doing three jobs: a face pulled at a child, a fright, and a fruit.

The Spanish Academy's first large dictionary, the Diccionario de Autoridades, gathers the same strands in 1729, in its second volume (S2). It lists the fruit with its three holes, a grub that spoils seeds, and a string of dark beads. It then gives the bogey: an ugly, frightening figure, or a cry like the monkey's, made to scare children and keep them in check. The Academy keeps the figure and the gesture side by side. The Coco is both a thing and something that is done to a child.

Why a figure with no face? The record gives no stated reason, only a pattern. A Portuguese eighteenth-century dictionary, read in chapter 6, says the scare works because it is a shape without order or proportion. The twentieth-century scholar M. Sandmann reports the Portuguese coco as a figure shown by hoods, masks and hollowed gourds with holes for eyes and a mouth (S5). Each of these is a face-shaped emptiness: a place where a face should be. A child can fill it with anything.

The nineteenth-century collector Francisco Rodríguez Marín printed a Spanish cradle song about the Coco in 1882. In a footnote he glossed the Coco as an imaginary being used to put fear into children, noted the saying "uglier than a coco", and listed other members of the same company, among them the duende and the cancón (S6). His footnote also quotes the Covarrubias entry, so by 1882 the dictionary and the nursery were already pointing at each other.

So the word was in the dictionaries by 1611 and in the footnotes by 1882. Where does a child first meet it? Not in a book. In a song meant to put him to sleep.

Hook: The word sits in a dictionary, but the figure lives in a lullaby, and the lullaby does not agree with itself.

#### ch-2 · The Song at the Cradle
anchor: el-coco--ch-2
sources: S6, S10
The lullaby is the Coco's real home. The earliest printed form read for this account is in Francisco Rodríguez Marín's Cantos populares españoles, the first volume of a collection published in 1882 (S6). Among the cradle songs in that volume is one that begins by addressing a small child, and says in short lines that the Coco is coming and will carry off the children who sleep too little. The verb is "carry off", not "eat". The threat is a consequence for staying awake, and nothing in the verse pictures a scene.

The same collector prints a cousin from Puerto Rico. It has "el cuco" in place of el coco, and its trigger is a child who cries a great deal. Spain had sent the word across the ocean and it had lost a letter, or gained one, on the way (S6).

The widely sung modern form is the one that says the Coco "will eat you". The sources read for this account give no such wording. The 1882 collection says "carries off". That version of the song, with the verb "eat" in it, is therefore listed as told, a later popular wording, and not as a documented text. A reference page online also repeats that the oldest known rhyme about the Coco comes from a seventeenth-century play by Juan Caxés, but the play's text was not found in a form that could be read, and the claim stays unchecked.

Portugal answers the Spanish song with a song of its own. Rodríguez Marín's footnote on the Coco goes on to say that the papão has the same job in Portugal, and he copies a Portuguese cradle verse that he credits to F. Adolpho Coelho's journal of ethnology and linguistics (S6). In the verse the mother does not threaten. She commands: go away, bogeyman, from over that roof, and let the boy sleep his rested sleep. The verse is not about a thing in the room. It is about a roof, tiles and a ridge, and a presence that sits on top of the house.

The roof returns in Brazil. Marina Mortoza's 2018 study of the Brazilian bogey Cuca, citing the folklorist Luís da Câmara Cascudo, prints three related verses, one Portuguese and two from Minas Gerais (S10). One of the Minas Gerais verses sends Coca away from the roof so the boy can sleep in peace. The Portuguese one sends the papão. In Brazil, the study adds, the Cuca is a regular character of lullabies, and a well-known one from the southeast has the mother say that Cuca will come to get the baby while the father is at the farm and the mother is out for a walk (S10).

Put the songs side by side and a pattern shows. In Spain the Coco is a threat that hangs over a child who will not sleep. In Portugal and Brazil the same mother speaks to the figure and tells it to leave the roof. The name changes from verse to verse: coco, cuco, coca, cuca, papão. The role stays. A lullaby is a bargain between a tired adult and the dark, and the dark is given a name so it can be spoken to.

None of the verses harms anyone. They end in sleep.

The song carries the figure down the generations. The next chapter shows what happened in 1799 when a painter took the figure out of the nursery and put it on a printed sheet for sale.

Hook: In February 1799 a Madrid perfume shop began selling a set of prints, and plate 3 carried the Coco's name.

#### ch-3 · The Print
anchor: el-coco--ch-3
sources: S7, S8, S9, S11
Francisco de Goya's Los Caprichos is a set of eighty etchings with aquatint, offered to the public in 1799 (S9, S12). Plate 3 carries the inscription "Que viene el Coco" at the bottom and the number 3 in the upper right corner (S7).

Two sources agree on the day of the first sale notice: 6 February 1799. A 1996 newspaper article on a facsimile edition made by the Calcografía Nacional says a long notice appeared that day in the Diario de Madrid, headed "Colección de estampas de asuntos caprichosos", and ended by saying that the set was sold at calle del Desengaño number 1, a perfume and liquor shop, at 320 reales de vellón a set of eighty prints (S8). The National Gallery of Victoria's essay on the series also names the Diario de Madrid and the date (S9). One university catalogue names the Gaceta de Madrid instead (S12). The article suggests that the shop may have held only one worn copy for public viewing, that buyers who showed real interest were sent on to Goya's own house, and that the first edition ran to about three hundred copies (S8).

The series did not stay on sale for long. The essay at the National Gallery of Victoria says Goya pulled it from public sale soon afterwards, and that in 1803 he gave the copper plates and 240 unsold sets to the Crown for the Real Calcografía (S9). The copperplate of Capricho 3 is now kept at the Calcografía Nacional, number 174 (S7).

The Fundación Goya en Aragón catalogue describes the print as an etching with burnished aquatint, 219 by 154 millimetres. Goya used aquatint for the dark background, and etching for the folds of a cloaked figure and for a woman and her children. Several trial states survive, none earlier than the aquatint. A preparatory drawing in the Prado is dated about 1797 to 1798 (S7). The Prado, which a reference page says opened in 1819, counts Goya as the most extensively represented artist in its collection (S11).

What did Goya mean? The catalogue lists three manuscript commentaries that explain the series, each paraphrased here. One, in the Prado, calls the print a criticism of bad early education, in which a child learns to fear imaginary figures more than his own father. Another, the Ayala manuscript, says that mothers use the Coco to frighten children into leaving them alone so they can meet their lovers. The third, in the National Library, mentions both the foolish mothers and the ones who want to be alone (S7). The catalogue also records a second reading: the print may point to thieves who exploited superstition by entering houses at night disguised as spirits, a reading supported by a passage in Feijóo's Theatro crítico universal (S7).

Note what Goya is not doing. He is not repeating the lullaby. The lullaby calls the Coco to the cradle to quiet a child. Goya, in the commentaries, laughs at the adult who calls him. The monster is the adult's tool, and the print aims at the adult.

That turn is why the sheet outlived its sale. By 1799 the Coco had a century and more of life as a word and a song, and now it had a famous picture.

Hook: A fruit sits on a windowsill in half the kitchens of the Iberian world, and its three dark marks look back at you. Which came first, the fruit or the fear?

#### ch-4 · A Fruit With a Face
anchor: el-coco--ch-4
sources: S1, S3, S4, S5
European travellers had long called the fruit only the nut of India. The name coco came with the Portuguese, and it is the same word as the bogey. Two old explanations compete.

The first comes from João de Barros, the Portuguese chronicler, in his Third Decade of Asia (S3). The edition read here is dated 1563; Sandmann dates the writing to about 1552 (S5). Barros describes the hard inner husk of the fruit. On it, he says, is a pointed mark that seems to imitate a nose, set between two round eyes from which the sprouts shoot when the nut wants to be born. By reason of that figure, which is not a figure, "our people", meaning the Portuguese, called the nut coco, a name put by women on anything with which they want to frighten children. The name stuck so firmly, Barros adds, that nobody knows another, though its proper names are tenga among the Malabar people and narle among the Kanarese (S3).

The second explanation is older in print. The Spanish chronicler Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, writing about 1526 according to Sandmann, says the fruit was called coco because the one hole, with two more above it, makes a gesture like that of a little monkey that seems to "coca", that is, to make a face (S5). Covarrubias in 1611 repeats the monkey tale in more detail: the Spaniards called the nut coco for the little gesture made by the three holes, which look like eyes and a mouth, because a coco is a face such as the monkey pulls when she is angry, with a throaty sound that goes "ko ko" (S1). The Diccionario de Autoridades in 1729 still cites Covarrubias on this (S2).

Sandmann, in a 1955 article in the Revista de Filología Española, weighs the two. He notes that Covarrubias credits the Spaniards, while in fact it was the Portuguese who met the fruit. He treats the monkey story as an etymological legend, born of a habit of inventing sources for words, and he gives weight to Barros. His conclusion is that the name of the fruit is Portuguese in origin, from the bogey word that frightens children, shown by hoods, masks and hollowed gourds with holes for eyes and mouth; the three holes of the nut look like that face. He rejects the other proposals, a South American word, a Greek word and an Indian myth, as unfounded (S5). Sandmann also notes that earlier Europeans had called the fruit only the nut of India, and that Garcia de Orta's 1563 colloquies still use that form (S5).

A century later, in 1712, the Portuguese priest Rafael Bluteau gave the word one entry for both senses (S4). Coco, or Coca, he writes, are words used to put fear in children, because the second shell of the coconut has on its surface three holes shaped like a skull. He then copies Barros's passage almost in full. The fruit and the fright are one word, and the dictionary keeps both.

The dispute is small, but it explains the object on the sill. Whether a monkey or a mask came first, the nut shows a face that is not a face, which is exactly how the bogey works. The word travelled in a ship's hold and settled in the nursery.

Hook: A Portuguese dictionary of 1712 does more than define the Coco. It tells the reader whether to scare a child with it.

#### ch-5 · Coca, Papão, Cuca
anchor: el-coco--ch-5
sources: S4, S6, S10
A name that crosses a border changes its clothes. The Coco of Castile has cousins in Portugal and across the Atlantic, and the sources read here show how close the family is.

In Portugal the word appears as Coca as well as coco. Bluteau's entry of 1712 begins with a cross-reference, "Coca, or Coco", and treats the two as one (S4). Rodríguez Marín's footnote, written in Spain in 1882, names a Portuguese equivalent: the papão, who has the same duty of frightening children (S6). The cradle verse that sends the papão off a roof is Portuguese. The same verse turns up in Brazil with the name changed to Coca (S10).

In Brazil the figure is Cuca. Mortoza's 2018 study, titled in English "Cuca, the Bogeywoman of Brasil", devotes most of its length to the words coco, coca and cuca, and presents Cuca as the most popular bogey of Brazil (S10). It finds her roots in the Iberian Peninsula. It records Cascudo's flat claim that coco, coca and cuca are one and the same being, and it shows that the Houaiss dictionary of Portuguese keeps the three words in overlapping fields of meaning: coca for a pumpkin and for a bogey, cuca for a head, and also, in Brazil, a way to speak of an old and ugly woman (S10). The pumpkin matters. A hollowed gourd with holes for eyes and a mouth is the same object as the empty face that Sandmann found behind the Portuguese coco, and a fruit-shaped head is a head.

The study adds that Cuca became a literary figure through Monteiro Lobato's children's book O Saci (1921), and a television star in the series Sítio do Pica-pau Amarelo (S10). Those later portraits are a separate matter. They are authored characters with fixed designs, and they are not the nursery figure, which has none.

Two details are worth a line each. In Galicia and in the Portuguese town of Monção, a festival dragon carries the same name, Coca, and shares nothing with the nursery figure except the word (S10). And the Diccionario de Autoridades of 1729 has its own entry Coca, with the sense of a processional serpent figure (S10). Those belong to a different story.

What survives across all these names is a pattern of three parts. A figure with no fixed face. A threat or a command in a lullaby. A household object that doubles as its mask: a hood, a gourd, a nut.

Language did the migration. No ship carried a bogeyman. A word was said at a cradle in Castile, in Lisbon, in Minas Gerais, and each mother filled it with what was close to hand.

#### ch-6 · The Coco Today
anchor: el-coco--ch-6
sources: S1, S2, S4, S6, S7, S10
The Coco never had a founder, and so it has never been owned. Each telling is a loan.

The oldest complaint is in the dictionary. Bluteau, in 1712, goes beyond defining the word and gives a short reflection on it (S4). Man, he writes, is made in the image of God and so naturally dislikes ugly things. That dislike is strongest in children, in whom reason is still weak, and it shows in the cocos and bogeys with which their nurses soothe them. Those bogeys, he says, are nothing but a shape without order or proportion, and children have no fear of figures that are shown well made and well proportioned. He then cites Faria's Notícias de Portugal for a certain modern author who mentions a country where nurses were not allowed to make cocos at the children, because children brought up on those fears grow timid and cowardly (S4). The scan of this last passage is partly cut. Goya's commentaries make the same complaint in different clothes, almost ninety years later: the adult is the problem, and the Coco is the tool (S7).

The word has also kept its everyday life. Rodríguez Marín, in 1882, recorded the comparison "uglier than a coco" as a saying (S6). The Academy's dictionary of 1729 already listed the grimace sense beside the bogey (S2), and Covarrubias had listed the verb to make cocos (S1). An old word that was a gesture, a face and a fright has kept all three jobs, which may be why it never needed a picture.

In Brazil the figure has become a literary and television character in Lobato's O Saci and the series Sítio do Pica-pau Amarelo, according to Mortoza, with a design of its own (S10). That design is the author's, and it is described here only by name. The same study shows that the folk Cuca lives on in lullabies, where she is still spoken to or about at the cradle (S10).

A last observation concerns what the figure lacks. No source read for this account gives the Coco a settled costume, a fixed colour, a voice or a history. Goya's print is the nearest thing to a canonical picture, and its commentaries disagree about what it means (S7). A figure without a face can be told in the dark by anyone with a hood, a sheet, a shadow on a wall or a gourd with three holes.

What this means for the modern reader is simple. The Coco is not a creature to be classified. It is a habit of speech. When a parent says a name to make a child behave, a Coco exists for as long as the name is said. When the adult stops, the Coco has nowhere to live.

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: el-coco--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10, S11
The record can be read in several places. Covarrubias's Tesoro of 1611 gives coco two entries, the fruit named after the grimace of the three holes, and the figure that frightens children in the speech of children (S1). The Diccionario de Autoridades of 1729 lists the bogey as an ugly, frightening figure or a monkey-like cry used to scare and restrain children (S2). Barros's Third Decade of Asia, in the 1563 edition, reports that the Portuguese named the fruit coco for its likeness to a face, a name that women used for anything that frightens children (S3). Bluteau's 1712 dictionary repeats Barros and adds the likeness to a skull (S4). Rodríguez Marín's 1882 collection prints a Spanish cradle song in which the Coco carries off children who sleep little, and a Portuguese verse that sends the papão off the roof (S6). Goya's Capricho 3 is the third plate of an eighty-plate series announced on 6 February 1799, with a preparatory drawing in the Prado and a copperplate at the Calcografía Nacional (S7, S8, S9).

Some things sit between the columns. Sandmann's reading of Oviedo, the date of about 1526 and the dating of Barros to about 1552 are taken from a scholar's article and not from Oviedo's own text (S5). The Prado's opening year of 1819 and the museum's coordinates come from a reference page (S11). Cascudo's words are taken from Mortoza's study and not from Cascudo (S10). The newspaper that carried Goya's notice is named differently in the sources, and the date is the same in all of them (S8, S9, S12).

The told side is large. That women invented the name coco to scare children is Barros's report, and it cannot be checked against any earlier record. That the coconut was named for a monkey's grimace is a tale that Sandmann treats as an etymological legend. That the Coco "eats" children is the wording of the modern song, and the documented song says "carries off". That coco, coca and cuca are a single being is a folklorist's claim. That Goya's Coco points to thieves in disguise is one reading, set beside two manuscript explanations in his own time. That there ever was a first Coco, a being that came before the word, is a story and not a source.

The rule is the same as for any story kept on paper. A claim that can be read on a page can be sourced. A claim that can be read only in the telling stays on the told side, however often it is repeated.

The method note is short. Where a dictionary entry and a reference page differ, the dictionary page was given the weight. Dictionary wording is paraphrased, and no quotation exceeds a few words.

What survives is a short list of things that hold the figure together: a word that had three jobs, a song that changes verbs from place to place, a printed sheet that turned the monster against the adult, and a fruit with three dark marks that look like a face. Take away any one and the others still stand. Take away the names and you are left with a hood in a corner of a room, and a child asleep.

Hook: On the whitewashed wall a shadow of a hood grows slowly, and the candle does not move.

The roof, the verb "carry off" and the mother's command belong to this account.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands in a candle-lit Spanish nursery at night, then in a shuttered Portuguese street under clay roof tiles, then in an engraver's workshop with a dictionary on a lectern. A hooded shadow rests somewhere on a wall.

**Scenes**

- `scene-nursery` (the nursery): From left to right, a whitewashed wall with a coat on a peg, an empty wooden cradle with a folded blanket, a stool with a candle, a window with its shutters half closed, a sill with a coconut, a small chair with a wooden toy horse, a corner where a hooded shadow falls across the wall. Mood: hushed, watched, calm. Light: one candle, warm gold, with long shadows on whitewash.
- `scene-street` (the street): From left to right, a doorway with a heavy bolt, a wall with a clay water jug, a row of shuttered windows, roof tiles against the night sky, a gourd with three holes set on a doorstep as a lantern, an iron bracket where a street lamp hangs, a dark side alley. Mood: sleeping town, a presence on the roofs. Light: one hung lantern and a pale moon.

Mood: quiet, careful, close. Light: a single oil lamp.

**Spirit encounter:** The Coco never speaks first and never shows a face. A tall hooded shape of shadow stands where a coat or a drape might hang, still, with a hollow where a face would be. It asks one question at a time in a low, sing-song voice, slow as a lullaby. A wrong answer dims the candle and lifts the fear meter, and the shape leans a little closer along the wall. A right answer makes the candle brighten, the hood lowers a hand's width and the shape draws back toward the corner. At the end it asks which account the visitor believes, by naming the single detail that belongs to it. No child appears at any time.

**Breaking the curse:** The final puzzle is the shape's last question. The visitor chooses, from a row of four tokens (a coconut marked with a nose between two eyes, a monkey's face, a copper plate and an empty cradle), the one that matches the true account: in v-barros the coconut with the nose; in v-monkey the monkey's face; in v-goya the copper plate; in v-cradle the empty cradle. A right answer lets the hood fall softly and the shape fold into an ordinary coat on a peg. The relic earned is a small coconut-shell night-light with three punched holes that throw a warm pattern of two eyes and a mouth on the wall, harmless and bright.

**Fear events** (never block progress; static fallback when reduced motion is on)
1. A slow lullaby hum is heard once, no words, from somewhere behind the wall.
2. The hooded shadow on the wall grows a hand's width and returns.
3. The candle flame leans toward the window and straightens.
4. A shutter creaks once.
5. Three small dots of warm light appear on the plaster, then fade.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Folklore and language, not religion or practice. No saint, procession or rite drives any chapter. The festival dragons that share the name Coca, and the processional sense in the 1729 dictionary, appear in one line only and are not described. The Algarve procession scarecrow in the Brazilian study is left out on purpose.
- The Coco is a nursery threat and stays one. "Carries off" and "will eat you" are quoted only as sung warnings, never shown, and no child is in danger in any image or scene. A cradle may be empty, or the child asleep and calm.
- Covarrubias's guess at the origin of the bogey word rests on a racially loaded association and is not supported by later sources. It is left out of the story, the facts and the images entirely. A neighbouring cradle song in S6 uses a figure drawn from an ethnic or religious group; it is left out.
- The Coco here is an original design: a hooded, faceless shadow, with no eyes and no mouth. Do not copy Goya's Capricho 3 composition (mood only), Monteiro Lobato's Cuca or the television series' design, or any animated film titled Coco, nor any other film, TV, comic or game Coco, Cuco or Cuca.
- Goya's print is from 1799 and in the public domain. The legend uses its history only; the plate's composition is not reproduced in any prompt.
- Items not checked against a primary text (tagged [R], never used in true-or-told slots): the Prado's opening year, the Lobato dating. The date of the notice is the same in three sources, but the newspaper is named differently (Diario de Madrid in S8 and S9, Gaceta de Madrid in S12); the chapters report both.
- The oldest known Coco rhyme in a seventeenth-century play is repeated online, but the play was not read, and nothing is built on it.
