## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] It is late. The turf has burned down to a red eye in the hearth. A kettle ticks as it cools. Nobody in the house is ill. The old man sleeps. The children sleep. Then, from the direction of the river, a sound comes. It is not a fox. It is not a cat. It is not the wind in the hawthorn, though it moves like wind. It is a long, low lament, like the funeral singing the neighbours still remember from their grandmothers' wakes. A woman at the table puts down her knitting and does not look at the window. She counts the nights since the last time. She does not say anything. In the morning, a boy on a bicycle will come up the lane with news from three parishes away. This is the story of the woman at the edge of the dark, the name Ireland gave her, and the very different people who have said they heard her.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · A woman of the mound
anchor: banshee--ch-1
sources: S1, S2, S5, S8, S14, S18, S19

Start with her name, because the name is already a small puzzle. In Irish she is the *bean sí*. English speakers flattened that into "banshee", and the spelling hides what the words say. *Bean* is "woman". *Sí* is the word for a fairy mound, the green rise in a field that farmers for generations were careful not to plough flat. The older form, Old Irish *ben síde*, means "woman of the fairy mound", or just "fairy woman". She belongs to the Otherworld, but she is attached to a specific family on this side of it.

That attachment is the heart of the legend. She is not a monster that hunts. She is not a ghost haunting a place. In the tellings, she announces. A cry is heard, usually at night and usually close to the house, shortly before a death in a family she follows. Several accounts agree on a cruel detail: the person who is dying does not hear her. Someone else in the family does.

Who gets a banshee? In the most repeated version, only the old Gaelic families, the ones with names beginning Ó or Mac. The surname was a kind of invitation. Hearing her was a mark of lineage, and that gave the story a strange pride alongside the grief. Patricia Lysaght, the University College Dublin ethnologist whose 1986 book *The Banshee: The Irish Supernatural Death-Messenger* is the standard scholarly study, described the cry as a kind of "intergenerational link" with ancestors, offering comfort and a sense of belonging. There are exceptions to the rule, and the exceptions are quietly interesting. In 1910 the Clare antiquarian Thomas Westropp, after the collector Thomas Crofton Croker, named families of old English stock with a banshee: Burkes, Rices, Husseys, Trants and Keatings, and the FitzGeralds, the Geraldines, of Kerry and Limerick. He added that his own family, from Yorkshire, had one in Clare. One later article says Norman settlers claimed a banshee to look more Gaelic. No original record of that motive has been found, so it stays on the told side.

Writers argued about where to draw the line. W. B. Yeats, in his 1888 collection, wrote in a note that she follows the old families "and none but them". Lady Wilde, in her 1887 collection, allowed more room: certain families of historic lineage, or persons gifted with music and song. She also gave the banshee several forms. Sometimes she is a sweet-singing young woman of the family who died young. Sometimes she is a shrouded woman crouched beneath trees, lamenting with a veiled face, or a figure flying past in the moonlight, crying. The people who actually told these stories on their own doorsteps rarely drew such lines.

Where did she come from? Nobody knows, and the scholars say so plainly. The best-known guess ties her to the old custom of keening, the paid and unpaid lament sung over the dead. Yeats's note ran the guess the other way: he wrote that the keen is said to imitate her cry. One of her other Irish names, *bean chaointe*, means "keening woman", and it is used in parts of Leinster. A different guess points at nature. A low booming call, like that of the bittern, a secretive wetland bird, has been suggested as the sound behind the story. Neither guess has been proved, and a good investigator keeps both.

Notice what is missing from her name: any word for a woman who kills. The banshee does not cause the death. She is only the first to know. That makes her the quietest sort of horror, the horror of being told something you cannot change.

The Irish countryside gave this figure plenty of places to stand: a river bank, a hawthorn, a gate, a window. In the next chapter, we walk into a kitchen where the other half of the story was still performed by living women, who rocked, wailed and sang over the dead for a fee, and who were taught never to do it anywhere else.

Hook: Before there was a banshee at the window, there were real women at the wake. What did they know that she knows?

#### ch-2 · The women who wailed for a living
anchor: banshee--ch-2
sources: S1, S3, S5, S13

Imagine a wake in a stone cottage a century or two ago. The body lies in the main room. The turf fire is lit. Neighbours fill the kitchen. Then the women arrive whose job it is to give grief a voice. They are the *mná chaointe*, the keening women, in the singular the *bean chaointe*. The Irish word for the lament itself is *caoineadh*, from a verb meaning "to cry, to weep".

Keening was not random weeping. It had a shape. The relatives and the keeners formed two groups, one near the head of the dead person and one near the foot. They sang alternately, and then joined in a full chorus. Folklorists describe three parts: a salutation to the dead, a verse-like dirge, and the *gol*, the open cry. The words were improvised, but they leaned on stock themes: the dead person's family line, their praise, and the hardship now facing those left behind. Between the words came wordless sounds, a moaning and rising that listeners remembered long after the words were forgotten. There was no fixed metre, only a rocking rhythm.

A writer of the twelfth century, Gerald of Wales, remarked on it. In his *Topography of Ireland*, in a passage on the effects of music, he says the Irish and the Spaniards mix plaintive music with their funeral wailings. That is one line, from an outsider, and it does not describe the two-group singing above; that comes from later writers. It does show the practice was already worth remarking on. In later centuries many keeners were hired, and they were paid for the service. They wore their hair loose and often went barefoot as a sign of mourning. They were also careful. According to accounts gathered by modern writers, professional keeners did not keen outside a funeral. To do so was considered improper and unlucky. A keening voice belonged to a death that had already happened.

That rule matters for the legend. If the keeners kept their singing for the dead, then a keening heard in the dark, with nobody dead, was a sound out of its place. It was the sound of the wake arriving early. Some storytellers resolved the puzzle in a simple way. Lysaght suggested that a woman who had been a professional keener in life might herself become a banshee after death, going on with her work as the family's lament. This is a folklore explanation, not a documented fact, and it explains the sound but not the figure's other forms.

The keening tradition faded. Writers describe it as declining over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. An RTÉ essay says the need for professional keeners died out by the middle of the twentieth century. A few recordings survive. One is a clip from 1949 of the keener Bríd Iarnáin from Inishmore, one of the Aran Islands. The National Museum of Ireland's Country Life collection in Mayo also holds material connected with the tradition.

That is how the story gets hard to pin down. The banshee may be a memory of a custom, or the custom may be a memory of the banshee, or neither. What is certain is that people in rural Ireland knew exactly what a keen sounded like. When they said the cry outside the house reminded them of it, they were comparing it to something they had heard with their own ears.

Hook: The keeners never sang for the living. So who is singing at the river tonight, and which family is she singing for?

#### ch-3 · Aibhill of the hill
anchor: banshee--ch-3
sources: S14, S15, S16, S10

Not every banshee is nameless. In Clare and the surrounding counties, one family had a banshee with a name: the O'Briens, descendants of the kings of Thomond. Her name appears in varying spellings, Aibhill, Aibhinn or Aoibheall. The Clare antiquarian Thomas Westropp, writing in 1910, thought Aibhinn may mean "the lovely one" and may once have been the goddess of the House of Cass. He placed her at Craglea, the great bluff above the Shannon gorge near Killaloe, where a well and a crag still carry her name. He also recorded the Clare belief that twenty-five other banshees call her their queen and always attend her.

The most famous story involves the Battle of Clontarf in 1014. It appears in *Cogadh Gaedhel re Gallaibh*, "The War of the Irish with the Foreigners", a text composed in the early twelfth century, about a century after the event. In it, on the day of the battle, the aged Brian Boru refuses to retreat. He says Aibhill of Craig Liath came to him the night before and told him he would be killed that day. The battle is real history. The visit from a banshee is the king's reported belief, and a careful reader files it under "told".

A second account is also medieval, set down within about thirty years of the events. *Caithréim Thoirdhealbhaigh*, "The Triumphs of Turlough", written about 1350 by the O'Brien family historian Seán mac Craith, describes the Battle of Dysert O'Dea in May 1318. In the text, Richard de Clare, leading an Anglo-Norman army toward Dysert, the seat of the O'Deas, comes to the River Fergus and sees a hideous old woman in the current, washing armour and rich clothes until the river runs red. One of the Gaelic allies with the army asks her whose gear it is. She answers that it is de Clare's and his men's, and that few of them will escape. She gives her name as the Water-doleful. The text does not call her Aibhill. By the next day de Clare, his son and most of his army were dead. The washing woman is in the text, and she belongs to the told side. Westropp also recorded a Dysert legend that gave her an entourage: Aibhill and twenty-five banshees washing blood-stained clothes in Rath Lake before de Clare fell.

Clare folk also gave the landscape a name. Westropp recorded that a stream from Caherminaun to Dough was called the Banshee's Brook, and he wrote that the banshee heard there was apparently not Aibhill. After an unusually dry summer, when the water ran red with iron scum, everyone listened for the rustling flight of a banshee and her attendants through the air. If no one died, the sign was quietly forgotten. This is how an omen survives: the hits are remembered and the misses are not.

The family that held the title Earls of Thomond made Bunratty Castle their headquarters in the later sixteenth century. According to Heritage Ireland, the castle was built for Maccon MacNamara in the fifteenth century, had passed to the O'Briens by the early sixteenth century, and the "Great Earl", Donough O'Brien, made it his headquarters from 1583. That is why this atlas puts its map pin there. It is a place the public can visit, tied by lineage to the family the banshee followed, and not a private home. It is not the place of Aibhill's crag, and it is not a place where any of these stories is known to have happened.

Still, notice the pattern. One banshee for one family. One crag. One brook. The story is neat, and stories that are neat are often made neat later. In the next chapter, a foreign visitor writes down what she saw in the middle of an Irish night, and nothing about it is neat at all.

Hook: A clan banshee rules a crag and twenty-five followers. But in the 1600s a visitor wrote about a woman at a window in an O'Brien house, and she had no crag at all.

#### ch-4 · The woman in the casement
anchor: banshee--ch-4
sources: S6, S14

Here is one of the earliest first-hand written accounts of a banshee-like figure that we can read for ourselves. It comes from the memoirs of Ann, Lady Fanshawe, an Englishwoman who wrote about her life for her son. She travelled in Ireland while Cromwell's army was campaigning there, around 1650. After Limerick, where they had been guests of the mayor and aldermen and then of Lord Inchiquin, she and her husband stayed three nights at the house of Lady Honor O'Brien, whom she describes as the youngest daughter of the Earl of Thomond, and as a lady who "went for a maid", which means she was thought unmarried, though Fanshawe adds that few believed it.

On the first night, around one o'clock, Fanshawe woke to a voice. She drew back the bed curtain. By moonlight, she saw a woman leaning in through the casement of the window. The woman was dressed in white, with red hair and a pale, ghastly face. She spoke loudly, in a tone Fanshawe had never heard, and said one word three times. The word was about a horse. Then she sighed, with a sound more like wind than breath, and vanished. Fanshawe wrote that the body looked more like a thick cloud than a solid shape. She said she was so frightened her hair stood on end. She tried to wake her husband, who did not stir at first, and when he did, they looked at the window together. Neither slept again.

At about five in the morning, the lady of the house came to the room. She said she had been awake all night, because a cousin of hers, an O'Brien whose ancestors had owned the house, had asked her to stay with him in his chamber. He had died at two o'clock. She apologised for the disturbance and explained that it was the custom of the place: when a member of the family was dying, the shape of a woman appeared at the window every night until the death. She then gave a sad explanation about a woman of the house in earlier ages and the river below the window. It is the household's own story of how the figure came to be, and the visitors could not check it. We will keep the explanation off-screen.

Fanshawe's own conclusion deserves careful reading. She and her husband agreed that apparitions like this were more common in Ireland than in England, and they put it down to the "great superstition" of the Irish. That is the voice of a Protestant English visitor in the 1600s, and it tells us as much about her as about what she saw. She was honest about the fright, and equally honest about her doubt.

Look at what the account does and does not say. It names the host, Lady Honor O'Brien. It does not name the house, the village or the county. Some later retellings fill the gap. Westropp, in 1910, wrote that it was at a castle, "perhaps Bunratty or Castle Lake", and he retold the scene with details the memoir does not have, such as a scream and a girl. The memoir itself does not say Bunratty. It also describes a woman who is not a keener and not an old woman at a ford. She is a pale figure at a window, tied to a house, not to a bloodline, and her cry is a word, not a lament. Compare her carefully with the banshee of the clans and with the keening woman of the wake. They share a function and almost nothing else.

Hook: At two in the morning a man died. At one, a woman in white had said a word about a horse. What was she asking for?

#### ch-5 · The washer at the ford
anchor: banshee--ch-5
sources: S2, S4, S5, S8, S16, S17

Cross the water and the legend changes shape. In Scotland, the nearest cousin of the banshee is the *bean nighe*, the washerwoman, also called the washer at the ford. She is a specific kind of *bean sìth*, the Scottish Gaelic spelling of the same name we began with. She is seen beside a stream or pool in a lonely place, washing the bloodied linen or grave-clothes of someone who is about to die.

The image is vivid and sorrowful, and its regional versions disagree with one another in telling ways. On Skye she is said to be a squat figure, like a small, pitiful child. In Perthshire she is small and round, dressed in green. In other places she has a single nostril, or one large front tooth, or red webbed feet. Some traditions say these washers are the spirits of women who died in childbirth, and that they must keep washing until the end of the natural lifespan they were denied. That is folklore. No record shows any such women, and it is best read as a story people told to give a sorrowful figure a sorrowful reason.

Why does this belong in an Irish atlas? Because the same figure turns up in Ireland. In the late tale of the death of Cú Chulainn, as Eleanor Hull retold it in 1898, the hero rides out toward his last fight. At the Ford of Washing near Emain he and his foster-father Cathbad meet a pale, yellow-haired maiden at the water's edge, wringing out crimson, bloody spoils. Cathbad says she is Badb's daughter, washing the hero's own gear because she foresees his fall. Cú Chulainn goes on anyway. The older Book of Leinster version, in Whitley Stokes's translation, has no such scene, so the washer belongs to the later telling. The Dysert story from the last chapter, with the washing woman in the River Fergus and the bloodied armour, is another medieval example. Some tellings also give the washer a wooden beetle, the tool for beating cloth on a stone. That detail has not been traced to an original source, so it stays on the told side.

Now compare this washer with the Irish keening woman. One is silent and busy with her hands. The other is all voice. One is seen at a ford, away from the house. The other is heard from the yard. One is tied to a coming battle or a violent end. The other tends to come before a death at home, in bed, among relatives. These are different jobs. A storyteller who says "the banshee was washing" is telling a different story from one who says "the banshee was keening", and a hasty investigator who mixes them will chase a witness who never existed.

Lysaght also noted something about direction. In her description, the sound of the banshee often comes along the river toward the home. The river brings her. That small detail links the ford and the house, and many witnesses describe both a stream and a threshold in one tale. Maybe that is why the legend feels like one story though it is several: water is the road she travels on.

It may also explain a tradition that the cry is heard abroad. Lady Wilde writes that the banshee follows the old race across the ocean. Her example is a branch of the O'Grady family in Canada. One night a bitter, wordless cry was heard outside their house, and next day the father and eldest son drowned in a boating accident. She calls it a well-authenticated instance, which is her word, not a record we can check.

Hook: The cry follows the river home. But what do ordinary families, in their own words, say they heard?

#### ch-6 · What the schoolchildren wrote down
anchor: banshee--ch-6
sources: S1, S5, S7, S12

Between 1937 and 1939, the Irish Folklore Commission, with the Department of Education, asked primary-school children across the Free State to collect folklore from their parents and grandparents and write it into copybooks. The result is the Schools' Collection, held by the National Folklore Collection at University College Dublin and now searchable on the website dúchas.ie. For students of the banshee, it is a treasure. It is not a collection of grand legends. It is a collection of what neighbours actually said about a woman crying in the night.

One account from Cromadh school in Croom, Co. Limerick, was written down in Corrabul. It comes from a parent, Mrs Devaney. She says the wail is loud and mournful, and sounds like the old funeral lament. It is mostly heard at night, though locally it was said to have been heard in broad daylight. It comes immediately before a death. Sometimes it is heard three nights in a row, and sometimes only once. The account names families the banshee was said to follow in the district, both humble and well-off. It names the Sheedys, a carter's family, and the Lyons, who were comfortable and had strong English connections. The writer notes that the Lyons were not very sympathetic to Irish people and customs. The record shows that the banshee did not respect surnames or money, whatever the grand writers claimed. There are many such pages, from many counties, and they are free to read. Four entries titled "The Banshee's Comb", for example, come from schools in counties such as Wexford and Offaly. In the Wexford one, two men take a comb as they pass, and the banshee follows them home and cries outside all night for it. A priest tells them to pass it back through the window on tongs. This is a told story, and it is one of the sources for the comb beliefs.

The sound in that account is loud and mournful, something like the keening of long ago. A modern writer in Ireland's national broadcaster's essay series adds that rural people knew the calls of foxes and cats well, and were unlikely to mistake them for the banshee.

How do people in the twenty-first century hear her? Quietly. Belief has faded as the older generation passed, rural electrification arrived, and parents who had moved away from country life stopped passing on stories they considered backward. Yet the figure persists. People retell family stories with her in them. Writers and tour guides return to her. The tale has moved from a warning to a heritage, from fear to a kind of belonging. Lysaght has said the wail's modern meaning is partly comfort: it links the living to the ancestors.

Press that point. In 1986, a television audience in Ireland reportedly heard a well-known scholar discuss the banshee and one audience member admitted to hearing her. Ordinary witnesses did not lose their stories because experts arrived. The experts arrived because the stories were still being told.

In our own telling, we do not ask you to believe in her. We ask you to read what people wrote. The pages are in their own hands, in their own words, with the names of the schools and the parishes attached. Then compare them with what the famous collectors wrote, and with the visitor's memoir at the window. Differences between the accounts are not mistakes in the legend. They are the legend.

One question is left, and it is the one a good documentary always saves for last: how much of all this can actually be pinned to a page?

Hook: We have the copybooks, the memoir and the annals. Now let us separate what is written down from what is only told.

#### ch-7 · What is documented, and what is told
anchor: banshee--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S5, S6, S7, S13, S14, S15, S16

We can now set the legend on a table and sort it. Start with what has a paper trail. The Irish name *bean sí*, from the Old Irish *ben síde*, "woman of the fairy mound", is documented language history. The custom of keening is documented too: its structure, its hired practitioners with their loose hair and bare feet, the 1949 recording of a keener from Inishmore, and Gerald of Wales's twelfth-century remark about funeral wailing. The memoir of Lady Fanshawe is a real text, written by a real visitor. Cogadh Gaedhel re Gallaibh and Caithréim Thoirdhealbhaigh are real medieval texts. Westropp's survey and Lady Wilde's collection are real books. The Schools' Collection is a real archive, and the Cromadh account is a real page. Patricia Lysaght's 1986 study is a real book. All of these tell us what people wrote, said or recorded.

Now the other side. That a woman exists who foretells death, that she follows a family through generations, that she lives at Craglea, that she rules twenty-five others, that she warned Brian Boru the night before Clontarf in 1014, that a washer at the River Fergus told de Clare's army that few would live: these are all told. The texts and the books prove only that people wrote them down. The battles are history. The woman is not in the battle record. Some of the best stories were set down decades or centuries after the events they describe. A careful reader keeps both halves in view and does not let the second borrow authority from the first.

There is a second sorting to do, between versions. The keening-woman version, the clan version, the window version and the washer version each have their own sources, their own era and their own habits. When one teller says "she was combing her hair", another "she was washing", a third "she cried a word at the window", and a fourth "she is Aibhill", they are telling different stories, and a detail true in one is false in another. The comb alone has two faces: one belief says never pick up a comb from the ground, and a schools' story has a banshee crying at the door for her comb. Know which story a witness is telling before you trust what they say.

A word about care. This legend sits close to real grief. Families in Ireland, and Irish families abroad, tell their own stories of hearing her before a loss. We treat those with respect. We do not offer rituals, charms or instructions for dealing with her, and we do not suggest she is real or unreal. We present folklore as folklore.

The last thing to say is the oddest. The banshee has survived every attempt to explain her. She may be a memory of the keening women. She may be a goddess of place whose name drifted. She may be a bird in a bog. She may be a way a family says what it cannot say aloud. A story that holds that much, for that long, is doing something for the people who tell it. It tells them they were known, that someone was keeping count, and that grief had a witness. The woman in the dark is not asking you to be afraid. She is asking whether you are listening.

Hook: The table is set. Choose your witness carefully.

#### versions--v-keener
anchor: banshee--versions--v-keener

In this version, the banshee is the keening woman made into a spirit. The tradition behind it is the ordinary one: a household loses a member, and paid keening women come to the wake, hair loose, often barefoot, to lead the lament. They are careful to keen only at a funeral. The banshee version says a woman who did this work in life might do it after death, for the family she once served, and might do it before the death, as a warning. Her cry is described like the old funeral song. A witness who tells this version will say she was heard, not seen, and that the sound was a lament with the shape of a wake. Lysaght suggested the link; it is a folklore explanation, and no document confirms it. Where it breaks: the keeners did not keen for the living, so a keening woman who sings before a death is breaking her own profession's rule. A witness who says "she was at the ford washing" is not telling this version.

#### versions--v-clan
anchor: banshee--versions--v-clan

In the clan version, the banshee is Aibhill, the figure of the O'Briens. She belongs to the crag of Craglea near Killaloe and is queen of twenty-five others. She speaks to kings. The best-known story has her appear to Brian Boru the night before Clontarf in 1014. This version is rich with place names: Craglea, Killaloe, the Banshee's Brook between Caherminaun and Dough, though Westropp says the brook's banshee was apparently not Aibhill. Witnesses who tell it will name the family, name the hill and speak in a courtly register. It is told, not documented; the events are history, and the banshee scenes were set down by writers a century or more later. Where it breaks: a clan banshee is a public figure with an entourage, yet the schools' witnesses speak of a single woman and ordinary families, and the visitor's account has a figure in a house, not a hill.

#### versions--v-window
anchor: banshee--versions--v-window

The window version comes from a visitor who wrote it down. In the memoir, Ann Fanshawe sleeps three nights at the house of Lady Honor O'Brien. At one o'clock on the first night, she sees a pale woman in white with red hair lean through the casement and speak one word, about a horse, three times. At two o'clock, a cousin of the host dies. The household explains that the figure appears at the window every night until a family member dies. A witness who tells this version names the colour of the hair, the dress, the hour and the word. Where it breaks: she is not old, she is not keening, she does not follow a bloodline to a hill, and the memoir does not name the house, so any witness who says "it happened at Bunratty" is repeating Westropp's guess, not the source.

#### versions--v-washer
anchor: banshee--versions--v-washer

In the washer version, the omen is something you see, not hear. A woman kneels at a ford, beating or wringing linen that is stained with blood, and the cloth is the clothing of someone who will soon die. Scottish tradition calls her the *bean nighe*; Irish tradition has an old form of her too. In the Caithréim, written about 1350, the Dysert washer of 1318, at the River Fergus, is a medieval example. Some tellings give her a wooden beetle for beating the washing; that detail is told and untraced. A witness who tells this version speaks of water, stones, wet cloth and a battle or violent end. Where it breaks: she does not wail, so any witness who gives a cry with her is mixing versions, and she belongs to the bank, not to the house.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** the player stands inside a stone cottage and its surroundings on a misty night, three paces from a river, in a valley of rushes and drystone walls. Nothing is closed, dangerous or real; it is a painted archive room.

### Scenes

**sc-kitchen: The peat-fire kitchen.** From left to right: a half door, a wall dresser with plates and jugs, a spinning wheel with a hank of grey wool, the open hearth with a crane and pot over the glowing turf, a wall clock, a settle bed with a sleeping cat, and a shawl hung on a wooden peg beside the back door. Mood: hushed, warm at the centre, cold at the edges. Light sources: the glowing turf fire and a faint blue seep of moonlight under the half door.

**sc-ford: The river ford at dusk.** From left to right: a gnarled hawthorn on the bank, a heron on a stone, a row of stepping stones, a flat washing stone with a heap of wet linen, a wooden beetle propped against it, and a stone wall fading into mist. Mood: lonely, damp, listening. Light sources: the last green light of dusk over the water and fog glow.

**sc-loft: The loft room.** From left to right: a plain iron bedstead, a small casement window with curtain, a wooden chest, a candle stub in a saucer, a small table with a bundle of papers, and a comb on the sill. Mood: close, still, held breath. Light sources: moonlight through the casement and a dying candle.

### Spirit encounter

She never walks in. First the player hears her through the world: a low lament that rises and falls like a funeral song. Then a pale shape forms in one scene's window or at the ford's far bank, always at the edge of the screen. She asks one question at a time, in the form of a gap: "Which of these is told, and which is written?" When the player answers wrongly, the fear meter rises by one step, the light dims, the lament deepens and she turns half away. When the player answers correctly, the meter falls, the mist thins and she turns a little toward the light, and a thread of her cry softens. The player never faces a death scene. Nothing is shown of harm. She only listens.

### Breaking the curse

The final puzzle (pz-5) asks the player to lay four objects on the hearth stone in the order of the true version for the seed: for v-keener a shawl, a plate, the order of the lament and a comb left in place; for v-window the casement mark, the white dress, the hour and the word; for v-clan the hill, the number, the date and the family; for v-washer the beetle, the green cloth, the ford and the year. A wrong mix of versions breaks the pattern. The relic earned is the **Lamp of Vigil**, a small iron rush-light holder. It does not banish her; it lets the player sit with her cry without fear and keep a record of what was heard.

### Fear events

1. A low lament drifts under the floor for a few seconds when a decoy is tapped. Sound only; never blocks input.
2. The fire dips to a thin red line and recovers. Under reduced motion this becomes a slow fade, not a flicker.
3. A pale shape appears at the far edge of the ford scene and is gone when the player pans toward it.
4. The loft candle bends toward the window for a moment.
5. A comb on the sill is seen to have moved one hand-width when the player returns to the scene.

All five are skippable, short, and replaced with a gentle fade and a subtitle when reduced motion is on.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Death omen: keep deaths off-screen. No coffins in view, no injuries, no blood. The red tint at the ford is iron-stained water, never blood.
- Fanshawe's household explanation involves a woman of the house killed long ago by its owner. The atlas does not retell it and does not use it as a puzzle driver.
- Folklore, not religion: no rituals, charms or "how to ward her off" are presented as real. The comb rule is reported only as a told belief.
- Grief: many families, and Irish families abroad, have their own banshee stories. Keep the tone respectful and never mock them.
- Surname purity: the "pure Milesian stock" idea is an old claim used to rank families. We report it as told and note that the Schools' Collection shows banshees following humble and comfortable families alike.
- Map point: Bunratty Castle is a public heritage site and is used only because of its O'Brien link. Westropp's 1910 guess that Fanshawe's house was "perhaps Bunratty" is not in the memoir, and the atlas does not claim it as the place of any event. No private homes are pinned. Players are not encouraged to visit any place.
- Do not copy film, TV, game or book banshee designs (for example screaming-woman horror portrayals, or a specific modern fantasy banshee). Our spirit is a mist-and-wool figure with her face hidden.
- Avoid generic "Celtic" clichés: no knotwork, harps, shamrocks or generic druids. Use drystone, thatch, wool, linen, creels and Hiberno-Romanesque chevron.
- Language: puzzle answers are numbers, simple names and Irish terms with plain-English glosses, to translate into Arabic later.
- Lysaght's 1986 book was not read directly; her views come from RTÉ and National Geographic interviews (S1, S5). The Caithréim, Cogadh, Yeats, Wilde, Fanshawe, Gerald and Westropp were read in their original or reprinted text. Wikipedia pages (S2, S3, S4) are leads only; claims resting on them alone (bean chaointe in Leinster, keening structure, bean nighe descriptions, the bittern idea) are not yet checked against originals.
