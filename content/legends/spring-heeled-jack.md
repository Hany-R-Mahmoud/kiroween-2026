## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] A clerk in a City office unfolds a newspaper on a winter morning in 1838 and reads a paragraph about a letter. The letter says that somebody rich has made a bet, and that a man in a costume is to visit the villages around London dressed as a ghost, a bear and a devil. The clerk laughs. By evening he is walking home a little faster than usual, and he glances at a dark rooftop. Across London, a thousand readers do the same. None of them can say who has seen the figure, only that a friend of a friend heard about it. The story is already bigger than anyone who has met it.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · A Bull, a Bear, a Rumour
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-1
sources: S1, S2
Before the figure had a name, it had no shape at all. The scholarly accounts agree that tales were passing between the villages on the south and west edge of London in the autumn of 1837 (S2). First the talk was of a phantom white bull, then of a white bear, and then of a tall, dark figure in a cloak who leapt out of the night and vanished again. The earliest tellings tie the figure to Barnes Common, a place with a bad local reputation for robberies, and the choice of such a spot made the story easier to believe (S2).

The newspapers of the time found it hard to pin down. A report in the Northampton Mercury of 30 December 1837 described Lewisham residents speaking of a figure in a bear skin and spring shoes that jumped to and fro in front of walkers, apparently as part of a wager (S1). In that same report the figure was said to be appearing in nine different parishes. Another early label, "Steel Jack", seems to have come from reports of a figure in armour (S1). Mike Dash, whose long study of the legend appeared in 1996, is cited as having found "Steel Jack" as the earliest name, and the scholars who build on his work accept it as a stepping stone to the later one (S1, S5).

A caution runs through all of this. The Morning Herald of 10 January 1838 sent a reporter to chase the tales to their sources. He was directed to people said to have suffered at the hands of the ghost, but each denied knowing anything and pointed to someone else, who also denied it (S1, S2). The police said they could not find one person bold enough to claim a first-hand account. The paper wrote that the stories were in everybody's mouth, but nobody who had seen the figure could be found (S2).

That detail is the key to the whole legend. A fear can spread without a single named witness, because every teller is passing on what somebody else said. Karl Bell describes this as the classic pattern of an urban legend: it happened to a friend of a friend, close enough to feel real and distant enough to need no proof (S2).

Bell also notes that the figure was not new in its raw materials. English villages already had ghost stories about bulls, bears and leaping spirits, and the older figure of the devil, a trickster with horns and a talent for tricks, was part of every child's picture of the world (S2). Mackley adds that earlier stories of "leaping" apparitions existed as far back as 1817 and that a report from Southampton in 1826 spoke of a masked person in armour who wore spring boots to vault a ten-foot wall (S1).

So in the early weeks the figure was a mood more than a person. It was a ghost to some, a prank to others, a bull or a bear when the tale reached a different village. What it lacked was a public moment, a date on which respectable people had to take it seriously.

That moment came from a letter and from a man whose job was to be sensible.

Hook: A Lord Mayor sits down at his house in the City to hear a complaint. What does the letter say, and what does it set off?

#### ch-2 · The Letter at the Mansion House
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-2
sources: S1, S2, S5, S6
The Mansion House is the official residence of the Lord Mayor of London (S6). In January 1838 the Lord Mayor was Sir John Cowan, and he held a public session there at which a letter was discussed. The letter was anonymous and signed "a resident of Peckham". The Times printed an account on Tuesday 9 January 1838 (S2, S5). The sources differ slightly on the day of the session itself: one says a representation was made on 8 January and discussed the next day, while another says the paper reported proceedings from the day before (S1, S2). The printed date, 9 January, is firm.

The letter, as the Times gave it, claimed that some people of high rank had made a bet with a reckless companion. He was to visit many villages near London in three disguises, a ghost, a bear and a devil, and he was to enter gentlemen's gardens to alarm the households (S1). Other papers repeated the story the next day, and one version had the ghost in polished steel armour (S1). The writer added that the bet had been taken up.

The Lord Mayor was not convinced. Reports say he treated the matter as probably exaggerated, and that he asked the police to inquire (S5). That attitude seems to have fitted the tone of the day: the papers expected readers to laugh. Bell argues that printing the account gave the rumour the weight of print and the dignity of an official hearing, and that this did the opposite of what a joke needs (S2).

Letters began arriving. One writer, Thomas Lott of Bow Lane, wrote to the Lord Mayor that a gentleman drove about in a cab with a servant in livery and, throwing off a cloak, appeared in frightful forms to win a wager (S1). A pile of letters from different parts of London was shown to the crowded room a day or two later, according to a modern summary of the press record (S5). By 20 January the Sun was reporting a group of aristocrats with a wager of five thousand pounds, and it added a list of targets and a story of placards offering a ten-pound reward, all without any support, as Bell notes (S2).

Think about what had happened. A rumour that lived in conversation had been written down, handed to an official, read out in a room and printed in a paper with a respectable name. Each step made it sound more real. Nothing in the file proved that a single masked man existed, but there was now a theme: a wager, rich men, disguises, villages near London.

The Morning Chronicle, according to Mackley, replied by running through reported "pranks" from one village to another and then added that a similar statement from Peckham would probably turn out as thin as the rest (S1). The paper expected the bubble to burst.

It did not. The legend was spreading in two directions at once. In the streets the story grew in the telling, and in print it grew in the reporting. The figure needed a face, and a name.

Hook: What did the letters and the papers say the figure looked like, and where was he supposed to have been?

#### ch-3 · A Costume Written in Newsprint
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-3
sources: S1, S2, S4, S5
Nobody can say how Spring-heeled Jack looked, because the descriptions changed from place to place and from paper to paper. A useful summary appeared in the Morning Chronicle of January 1838, and Mackley quotes it at length (S1). It listed a string of villages and what each reported. At Barnes the figure was "a large white bull". At East Sheen it was a white bear. At Ham and Petersham it was a devilish imp, and people there were said to go out with a lantern and a stout stick. At Hampton Wick and Hampton Court it wore armour of polished brass, spring shoes and clawed gloves. At Hammersmith and Kensington it wore armour, and children were said to have seen an unearthly being dancing by moonlight on the green in front of Kensington Palace (S1).

The reports about Clapham deserve a note. A story of an October 1837 encounter on Clapham Common is told in books and online, but Mackley states that these incidents are not documented in any contemporary source (S1). The Clapham tale belongs to the later telling.

The costume that most readers picture came together in stages, and two February reports supplied most of it. On 22 February 1838 the Times printed that Jane Alsop had reported a frightening encounter near Old Ford, and the Morning Post of 7 March printed that Lucy Scales had reported a frightening encounter in Limehouse (S1). Both are cited here only for how the figure was described. The first report gave a large helmet, a tight pale dress that looked like white oilskin, blue and white flame from the mouth, and eyes like red balls of fire (S1). The second gave a tall, thin figure in a large cloak, carrying a lamp like a policeman's bull's-eye (S1). A police officer quoted in the second report said that such flame could be produced by blowing through a tube containing spirits of wine and sulphur (S1).

The name came last. The press had been using "Steel Jack" and similar labels. Mackley dates the phrase "Spring-heeled Jack" in print to the West Kent Guardian of 13 January 1838 and The Times of 22 February 1838, although the January article, as quoted, calls him "Spring John" (S1). The exact first printing of the full phrase is not firmly settled in the sources used here. The Oxford English Dictionary, as summarised by David Clarke, defines the name as one for a person known for great activity in running or jumping (S4).

Bell suggests that "Jack" itself helped: it was the everyman name of chapbook heroes, tricksters and giant-killers, and commentators linked the leaping to the owner of seven-league boots (S2). A name with a built-in boy's-story flavour is easy to repeat and hard to take entirely seriously.

By the end of February the figure had a name, a costume, a manner of escape and a story about a bet. It had also been talked about in a hundred places by people who had not seen it.

Hook: With the name in print, who was copying it, and where?

#### ch-4 · Stage, Street and Imitators
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-4
sources: S1, S2, S3
Once a name has been printed, other people use it. On Monday 26 February 1838, the first melodrama about the figure was staged at the Royal Pavilion Theatre, with a Mr Graham in the title part, as an advertisement in the Satirist newspaper recorded (S1). The same week, the papers were still printing the reports from the magistrates' offices. A second play was later staged at a West End theatre, and in 1840 a play by John T. Haines about a figure of this name reached several theatres and travelling shows (S1, S2).

Imitators appeared quickly. The Examiner of 25 March 1838 reported that a young man had been discharged after saying he had only been playing at Spring-heeled Jack (S1). A report of 4 April 1838 told of a man fined £4 after his voice was recognised, with a warning from the bench that such imitators would find themselves in Newgate (S1). In these cases the "ghost" turned out to be a person, and the court treated the copying as a nuisance.

Elsewhere the figure drifted into local talk. The Bristol Mercury said in June 1838 that Jack had left London and was now visiting the distant parts of the country (S2). A Norwich street ballad of the early 1850s joked that some said he was black, some white, some short, some tall, and many saw nothing at all (S2). In the Black Country he was said to be seen on the roofs of public houses and churches, and clergy used his name as a warning against drink (S2). In Norwich, parents used it to get children home before dark (S2). In a Bicester court in 1861 a man tried to blame him for a fire he had set himself, without success (S2).

Henry Mayhew recorded another route in 1861. A Punch and Judy showman told him that the figure of the devil was commonly called "Spring-heeled Jack" and was chained up, and that he came up at the end to show himself to Punch (S3). A name that began in a Lord Mayor's courtroom had reached the booth on the street corner.

The pattern is clear. A press report gave the figure a name, the theatre gave it a stage, the street gave it a joke, and the courts gave it excuses. Each of these uses made the character less like a mystery and more like a type: a masked, leaping, flame-breathing prankster. Nobody needed to have seen him for him to be known.

A different suspicion also began to circulate in these years. Rumour looked for a gentleman behind the mask, and one name kept returning. The cheap printed serials that followed would make use of it.

Hook: The printing presses were now cheap enough to give Jack a whole book. What kind of figure did the serials make of him?

#### ch-5 · The Penny Dreadful Jack and a Name on the Told Side
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-5
sources: S1, S2, S4
Cheap weekly serials turned Spring-heeled Jack from a rumour into a hero. Mackley and Bell describe a long penny serial, Spring-Heel'd Jack: The Terror of London, produced by the Newsagents Publishing Company, dated 1863 by one scholar and reprinted around 1867 (S1, S2). The first-issue blurb admitted that a person known to the police as Spring-Heeled Jack had once caused alarm, then promised the "daring deeds" of a wonderful man in weekly numbers (S1, S2). In the story he is a nobleman with a hidden past who helps the weak, wears a mask, and uses a reversible cloak, black for the shadows and white for confrontations (S2).

The serial's own pictures are not one fixed design. Bell notes that illustrations in the penny serials show him hairy like a wolf, or like a bat, or simply diabolical (S2). That variety is important: the figure belonged to no single artist.

Other serials followed. A series published from about April 1878 and reprinted in the Boy's Standard in 1885 introduced a baronet's son named Jack Dacre as the man behind the mask, and told an invented origin for the springs (S1). A 48-part serial by Charlton Lea (Alfred Burrage) appeared in 1886, and a 12-part serial with the same title came from the Aldine Publishing Company in 1904 (S1, S2). Bell observes that by the time of the later serials the character had been explained so thoroughly that his mystery was gone (S2).

The serials also repeated a suspicion that writers had been hinting at for years. This sits on the told side only, and it concerns a real person. Later writers named Henry de la Poer Beresford, third Marquess of Waterford, a young nobleman with a rowdy reputation who died in 1859, as the man behind the mask. The sources used here find nothing to support it. An 1884 article in All the Year Round, quoted by Mackley, said that not a shadow of proof could be given for the theory, and Brewer's 1896 handbook repeats the story without evidence (S1). Bell calls the idea a suspicion based on little more than a bad reputation (S2). The 1878 serial itself argues against it: it points out that the Marquess was in a courtroom elsewhere at one of the dates of the early reports, and uses that as proof that the two were not the same (S1). The claim is an unproven later story about a real person, and nothing in these pages says he did it.

David Clarke notes that the serial figure was often a vengeful hero with horns and bat-like wings, and that Jack characters appear in penny serials between 1860 and 1904 (S4). The cheap press had taken a fear from the streets and sold it back as a thrill.

The same press kept the real reports in view. Soldiers and sentries were about to become the next audience for the legend.

Hook: In 1877 the leaper turned up at an army camp. What did the sentries say they saw?

#### ch-6 · Sentries, Rooftops and a Fading Name
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-6
sources: S1, S2, S8
The 1870s brought a revival. The name was linked to a "Peckham Ghost" in 1872 and a "Park Ghost" in Sheffield in 1873 (S2). Beatrix Potter's journal for 1 March 1877 describes a nuisance around Manchester since Christmas: young men calling themselves Spring-heeled Jacks, going about at dusk in costumes with lanterns and springs in their boots (S1). She wondered whether they were students, and her guess shows how the name had become a costume anyone could wear.

In March and April 1877 newspapers reported rumours about sentries at the remote posts of the Aldershot army camp. Sheldrake's Aldershot and Sandhurst Military Gazette of 17 March 1877 told of a figure that dodged about a sentry box and made off at astonishing speed (S1). The Times of 28 April 1877 reported a figure that came close to a sentry and then leapt away with great bounds (S2). Rifles were said to have no effect on it. The Times suspected that an officer was playing a trick, and the later Colchester report of December 1878 seemed to confirm that the leaper there was a junior officer (S2). Aldershot gave the legend a final burst of publicity. In the autumn of 1877 the Illustrated Police News carried a picture of the figure at Newport Arch in Lincoln, wearing a sheepskin and escaping a crowd by leaping, according to a local history page (S8, reported).

After that the name thinned. From the autumn of 1888 the press turned to other fears, and the old leaper seemed almost childish beside them, in Bell's account (S2). Hoaxers were caught more often, and each unmasking removed a little more mystery. By the 1890s the name was linked with debates about penny serials and boys' reading, and later it became a general word for a nimble person or a fleet-footed thief (S2, S4).

The last traditional report came from Everton, Liverpool. In September 1904, according to Bell, the figure was said to bound down William Henry Street over several nights and vanish across the terrace roofs (S2). The same year saw the final Aldine serial, which ended after twelve parts (S2). The leaps in the stories had grown with each retelling. Earlier accounts had him clear a churchyard wall or a hedge, and by 1904 he was said to clear rooftops (S2).

In the twentieth century the legend rested for a time. David Clarke notes that most people had forgotten the Victorian reports by the First World War and that later writers revived the story in the mid-twentieth century (S4). Bell mentions a masked leaping figure in Provincetown, Massachusetts, between 1938 and 1945, called the Black Flash (S2). Modern books, television, comics and interactive entertainment have since given him a dozen looks, so every modern picture is someone's design; the Victorian record gives only a cloak, a helmet, a lamp, flames and a leap.

Hook: How much of all this can be pointed to on a printed page, and how much was only ever repeated aloud?

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: spring-heeled-jack--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S8
Every legend that grows in the newspapers has a paper trail, and the trail shows where the facts end.

On the documented side, a letter signed "a resident of Peckham" was discussed by Sir John Cowan, Lord Mayor, at the Mansion House, and the Times printed it on 9 January 1838 (S1, S2, S5). It described a wager and three disguises: a ghost, a bear and a devil (S1). Thomas Lott of Bow Lane wrote a further letter, and the Sun of 20 January named a wager of five thousand pounds (S1, S2). The Morning Herald of 10 January 1838 recorded that its reporter could find no first-hand witness (S2). The Times of 22 February 1838 and the Morning Post of 7 March 1838 carried the two fullest descriptions, and a police officer in the second suggested that a tube with spirits of wine and sulphur could make the flames (S1). The Royal Pavilion Theatre staged a melodrama on 26 February 1838 (S1). Punch showmen were using the name for their devil by 1861, as Mayhew wrote (S3). Serials with the title Spring-Heel'd Jack appeared from 1863, with later ones in 1878, 1886 and 1904 (S1, S2). Reports from Aldershot appeared in March and April 1877, and the last traditional report came from Everton in September 1904 (S1, S2).

Some details sit between the columns. The Newport Arch picture of 1877 is known here only through reference pages, and the dates of the wider claims in press summaries have not been checked against the original newspaper pages. They are reported, not confirmed (S5, S8).

On the told side, the Marquess of Waterford story is a later claim with no proof, and it concerns a real person. The idea that a group of aristocrats made a bet to frighten people to death is a newspaper claim without support (S2). The Clapham Common tale of October 1837, the leaps over roofs and mail coaches, the Duke of Wellington leading a hunt for him, and the figure as a kangaroo or an escaped inventor all belong to rumour and later retelling (S1, S2). That the figure was a ghost or a devil is a belief, not a record.

A last point on method. The sources disagree on small things: the day of the Mansion House session, the first print of the name, the date of some reports. Where they disagree, each account is given with its source and none is called correct.

What survives is a pattern more than a person. A fear moved from mouth to letter, from letter to newspaper, from paper to stage and from stage to serial. At each step someone added a detail. The story is about how a figure gets made.

Hook: The street is quiet now, but a tall shape still stands on a rooftop against the fog, far away, waiting to be reported.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands in a foggy Victorian lane at night, then in front of the Mansion House at dawn with newspaper stands whose pages are blank, then on a row of rooftops seen from a street. A tall cloaked silhouette stands far away, always out of reach.

**Scenes**

- `scene-lane` (a gas-lit lane at night): From left to right, a gas lamp with a halo in the fog, a covered cab with its lamps shaded, a shop window with a pair of long-soled boots, a brick wall with chalk marks, a playbill board with blank sheets, a dropped grey cloak on an iron railing, a corner with the faint shape of a leap against the sky. Mood: uneasy, quiet. Light: gas lamps with amber halos and a cold blue glint far down the lane.
- `scene-mansion` (the Mansion House steps at first light): From left to right, a newspaper stand with blank pages, stone steps and columns, a writing desk by an open door with a folded letter, a stack of letters tied with string, a policeman's lantern on a ledge, a clock face with no numerals, a pie cart. Mood: expectant. Light: pale dawn, one lamp at the desk.
- `scene-roofs` (a terrace of roofs seen from a street): From left to right, chimney pots in a row, a weathervane, a sentry box beside a wall, a bundle of penny serials on a crate, a Punch booth with its curtain drawn, a sheepskin draped on a post, a distant small silhouette mid-leap. Mood: watchful. Light: a low moon behind cloud and one lamp on the street.

**Spirit encounter:** The figure never comes near. A tall silhouette in a long cloak stands on a rooftop at a great distance, its head a faint dark shape with two pale blue points of light. It does not speak. Questions come as folded newspaper slips that blow along the lane, each ending in a question about what was printed and when. A wrong answer makes the fog thicken and the fear meter rise with a distant hollow thud, like a leap. A right answer clears a patch of fog and the silhouette steps a little further away. At the end it leaps out of sight and the slips fall still.

**Breaking the curse:** The final puzzle is the figure's last slip: the player must sort a short stack of statements into "printed" and "told" for the true version. Whichever version the seed picks, a right sort lets the silhouette rise into the fog and the lane grows quiet. The relic earned is a small brass bull's-eye lantern with a folded blank newspaper slip tied to its handle, cold and unlit.

**Fear events** (never block progress; static fallback when reduced motion is on)
1. A distant dull thud, like a landing, once.
2. A gas lamp flickers and a small blue glint appears at the far end of the lane.
3. A newspaper slip rustles across the cobbles and stops at the player's feet.
4. A silhouette crosses the sky above the rooftops and is gone.
5. The fog thickens briefly at the edges of the screen and clears.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Media panic legend, not a record of crimes. The two 1838 reports from young women (Jane Alsop and Lucy Scales) are each given as "a frightening encounter", with no injury detail and no reconstruction of an attacker. Later books add named victims and deaths (including a Bermondsey tale and others) that scholars say have no record; none of these are used.
- The Marquess of Waterford claim is stated only on the told side, labelled an unproven later claim about a real person, and nobody is named as the culprit. The version v-marquess is excluded from the seed's true-version pool: it can only ever be a dead end. The 1878 serial and an 1884 magazine are given as arguing against it.
- Do not copy any penny-dreadful cover or illustration (the 1863 and 1867 Newsagents Publishing Company serial, the Boy's Standard, the Aldine covers), or any film, TV, comic or game version of the figure. The spirit in this file is an original design: a far-off cloaked silhouette with no horns, wings, armour, crimson suit or flames.
- No fire-breathing is shown as a weapon; blue flame appears only as a faint glow.
- Map point is the Mansion House, a public building in the City of London. Do not suggest visiting commons, rooftops, army camps or any private or closed place at night.
- Items not checked against an original page (tagged [R], never used in true-or-told slots): the Newport Arch picture of 1877. Press dates quoted through Mackley and Bell have not been read in the original newspaper pages.
