**Why this map_point:** Baba Yaga has no home. Tales put her in "the deep forest" of any region, so no village or forest can honestly be called hers. ## 1. Cold open

You are sent for fire. Only that. A stepmother wants a light for the stove, so you walk into a forest where the trees lean closer the further you go. Three riders pass you: one white, one red, one black. You do not ask who they are. Then you see the fence. It is made of bones. On the posts, skulls look out with empty eyes. The gate has feet. The lock has teeth. Behind it stands a hut, turning slowly, standing up on legs like a hen's. From somewhere in the trees comes the sound of a mortar scraping through leaves, and a broom sweeping away its own tracks. The old woman has arrived. She sniffs the air. She says there is a visitor. She says there will be work. And before the day ends, she will ask you one question that decides everything.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · The Fence and the Hut
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-1
sources: S5, S8
[Dramatization] The clearing is quiet in the wrong way. No bird sings. In the middle stands a house, but not a house the way a village builds one. It rests on legs, thin and scaled and clawed, and every so often it shifts its weight like a sleeper turning over.

Start with the picture, because everything else grows from it. In the tale "Vasilisa the Beautiful," collected by Alexander Afanasyev, the girl reaches a hut in a forest clearing. Around it runs a fence made of human bones. On the stakes sit skulls, and in the dark their eye sockets glow, lighting up the whole clearing. The tale then goes further: the doorposts are legs, the bolts are hands, and the lock is a mouth full of sharp teeth. Reading it, a listener learns the first rule of the place. Nothing here is what it seems, and every part of the house is alive and watching.

Next comes the old woman herself. She does not walk to her home. She arrives travelling in a mortar, the heavy bowl used to grind grain, steering it with a pestle and sweeping away her own tracks behind her with a broom. The mortar and pestle are tools of a kitchen, so the picture mixes a housewife with a flying terror. Reference works credit the folklorist Andrey Toporkov with a suggestion that the pestle may have been her original tool, since it can also serve as a weapon, and that the mortar was added later by association. His article itself has not been traced, so this stays on the told side as a scholar's guess, not a record, and the atlas keeps the difference visible.

The hut on its legs became her trademark. It turns to face visitors, and the phrase that makes it turn can be read in the printed tales. In Afanasyev's tale "Baba-Yaga" (number 102), a man brings his stepdaughter to the hut and says, in short, "Hut, hut, stand with your back to the forest and your front to me." The hut then turns and lets him in. "Vasilisa the Beautiful" does not use the phrase at all, so it belongs to this other tale. Another tale, "The Maiden Tsar," describes a hut that turns on chicken legs in an open field, without any spoken phrase. What the tales share is the picture: a house that stands, and turns, and decides whether to let you in.

Why does it matter that the house is a creature? Because in this world, the first test is not a riddle. It is how you behave in front of something frightening. Vasilisa does not scream. She does not run. She stays, and she is polite. That behaviour is the key to the entire legend, and the old woman pays attention to it.

Notice also what the fence is made of, and what it is not. The tales never say who the bones belonged to. They are decoration, a warning, and a promise: this house takes its work seriously. A careful reader should not decide the tale is a murder story. It is a threshold story. You cross from the world of villages into a place with different rules, and the bones are the sign on the gate.

Light matters too. Vasilisa lights a splinter from one of the skulls to get fire into the oven. The skulls are both the warning and the thing the visitor came for. The fence guards the fire. The fire is what everyone wants.

Hook: The old woman has not yet said her name in the tale, but the girl knows it. What is she called, where was it first written down, and how old is the word itself?

#### ch-2 · The Grandmother with Two Names
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-2
sources: S2, S1, S10
The name comes in two halves. "Baba" means "grandmother" or "old woman" in several Slavic languages, and in Russian and Polish it can also be a rude word for an old, foolish woman. "Yaga" is the harder half. Its origin is unclear. Afanasyev himself proposed a link to a root meaning "serpent," and related words in other Slavic languages mean "witch," such as Old Czech jězě and Polish jędza. Scholars have not settled the question, and we do not pretend they have.

When did she first appear in writing? Reference works say the earliest mention is in Mikhail Lomonosov's Russian Grammar, whose title page carries the year 1755 although it was printed in 1757, and some add that the name appears twice. The passage has not been located in the printed grammar, and the scholarly book cited for it has not been checked. So the atlas treats this first mention as told, not documented. It is worth remembering how small any such mention would be: a short, dry line in a grammar book. The legend was already known well before anyone wrote her story down.

For that, the world waited for collectors. Alexander Afanasyev, a Russian folklorist born in the town of Boguchar in 1826, published his Russian Folk Tales (Narodnye russkie skazki) in several issues from the 1850s into the 1860s. Reference works disagree on the exact span, some saying 1855 to 1863, others 1864 or 1867, so this atlas does not lean on a single end date. Reference works also say he gathered nearly 600 East Slavic and Russian tales and drew inspiration from the Brothers Grimm, but that count has not been checked against the original, so it is told too. He died in 1871, aged 45. Reference works list seven of his tales with a Baba Yaga character, including "Vasilisa the Beautiful," "Marya Morevna," and "The Maiden Tsar."

A word on ambiguity. The scholar Andreas Johns, in his 2004 book Baba Yaga: The Ambiguous Mother and Witch of the Russian Folktale (Peter Lang), sorts the tales into three kinds, according to a published review of the book: the Baba Yaga who gives gifts, the ambiguous one, and the evil one. In the first she is a donor. In the last she is a villain. In the middle, she is both at once, which is where the best stories live. The same review says Johns reads the evil kind as a stand-in for the mother-in-law. Another scholar, Vladimir Propp, observed that her appearances across tales do not add up to one coherent picture. In other words, there was never a single, official Baba Yaga. There were many tellers, and each shaped her to the story at hand.

That is what makes her frightening. A villain you can predict. A tester who sometimes helps and sometimes threatens must be read carefully before you answer her, because you cannot tell at the gate which Baba Yaga you have met.

She has relatives, too. Westward, the Czech and Slovak Ježibaba and Polish Baba Jaga are related names. In South Slavic lands, a figure called Roga is used to frighten children. These are neighbours, not copies, and the atlas will not claim they are the same being.

Hook: Names and dates tell us she was known. They do not tell us what it was like to stand in her house. For that, we go back to the girl at the gate, and the price of fire.

#### ch-3 · Three Chores Before Dark
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-3
sources: S5
[Dramatization] The first night, the girl lies down in the old woman's house and does not sleep. Outside, the fence ticks as the wind finds its bones.

Here the legend turns from a picture into a test. In Vasilisa's story, Baba Yaga agrees to give the girl fire on one condition: she must work, or be eaten. That "or be eaten" is a threat, but the tale handles it lightly. It is spoken, never shown. We keep it the same way.

The old woman eats and drinks a huge amount, leaving the girl only a little soup, a crust, and a scrap of pork. Then she leaves a list for the next day. Sweep the yard and the room, cook the dinner, do the washing, fetch a measure of oats from the field and sift it. Everything must be done by the time she returns. Then she goes to sleep.

The girl, who has a small doll in her pocket, feeds it, and tells it her troubles. The doll's answer is gentle: lie down, morning is wiser than evening. By dawn the oats have been sifted. The doll, given by the girl's dying mother, is a helper, not a weapon, and it works only when fed first. Remember that order. Food first, then help. The tale never breaks it.

The second day, the old woman comes back, inspects the work, and is annoyed she can find nothing wrong. She calls three pairs of invisible hands, which carry the oats away to be stored. Then she sets a second task: clean a load of hay of all soil and every ear of grain, because somebody has mixed earth into it. That is an impossible-sounding job, and again the doll does it. On the third day the task is to prepare poppy oil, and the invisible hands take the poppies away when they are gathered.

Count the tasks the way this telling gives them, not the way a summary does. There is a first list on day one (sweep, cook, wash, sift oats), a second job (clean the hay of soil and grain), and a third (poppy oil). Other translations change the grain, reorder the days, or fold two tasks into one. This atlas follows Leonard Magnus's 1916 English translation, and every detail here comes from it.

What is the task for? That is the whole question. Is Baba Yaga cruel, or is she checking whether the girl can do what she cannot do alone? The tale lets you decide. The old woman is never praised in it. But she is also never defeated. She sets rules, watches, and keeps them.

Look at what the girl actually does. She is given work that cannot be done by force. She feeds her helper, rests, and trusts that morning is wiser than evening. Those are quiet, patient acts. In Baba Yaga's house, patience is a kind of courage.

Hook: Over supper on the last night, Vasilisa asks about three riders. The answer is what the tale has been hiding all along.

#### ch-4 · Day, Sun, and Night
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-4
sources: S5, S4
At supper, the girl, who has stayed quiet through it all, asks about the three riders she met on the road. Baba Yaga explains. The white rider is the bright day, the red rider is the red sun, the black rider is the dark night. She calls all three her faithful servants. Translations differ in the wording; one gives "Bright Dawn," "Round Sun," and "Dark Night," another uses Day, Sun, Night. The colours and the order are the stable part of the picture.

Look at what this does to our understanding of her. A woman who commands day, sun and night is not a petty witch in a hut. She is something larger, a keeper of time. Yet she lives in a clearing, sweeps with a broom, and cooks soup. The legend refuses to choose between the grand and the homely. That is exactly what Johns calls ambiguity.

The riders pass the girl in order as she walks: the white one brings daylight, the red one brings the sunrise, and the black one arrives at the hut as night falls. One summary says the skulls' eyes then glow in the dark, lighting the clearing. Notice how this ties the whole story to a single day and night. The fence lights up only after the black rider passes.

Then comes the surprise. The girl thinks of the three pairs of invisible hands, but says nothing about them. Baba Yaga warns that she eats anyone who is too curious. That is the tale's small, sharp lesson in what to ask and what to keep silent. Some questions are allowed. Some are dangerous. The girl chose well.

And then the question that matters: how did you do the work? Vasilisa answers that it was her mother's blessing. The old woman's reply is one of the strangest lines in folklore. Nobody who is blessed can stay with her. She pushes the girl out through the gate and gives her a skull on a staff, to carry home as fire.

The skull lights the way through the forest and fades at dawn. Back at the stepmother's house, it speaks, and its fiery eyes follow the stepmother and her daughters until they burn to cinders. We keep that outcome as the tale tells it: told once, not described, and never made graphic. The girl buries the skull and goes into town.

There is a reading of this ending that makes Baba Yaga a kind of judge. She does not punish the girl, because the girl passed. She does not feed the girl to the oven, because the girl was kind. What she hands over is the thing that was asked for: fire. The fire does what it does to the people who sent the girl into the forest. A cautious reader will note that this is an interpretation. The tale itself offers no comment.

Afanasyev read the story as a nature allegory, sunlight against storm, with Vasilisa as light and the stepmother and stepsisters as dark clouds. Other readers prefer a coming-of-age story. We do not need to pick.

Hook: In another tale, Baba Yaga is not one woman. She is three sisters, and the third one does not let her guest go.

#### ch-5 · Three Sisters and a Hungry One
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-5
sources: S8, S10, S2
In the Afanasyev tale "The Maiden Tsar," there are three Baba Yaga sisters. The hero, Ivan, visits each in turn. The first two receive him. At the third, the youngest sister goes off to sharpen her teeth for the unexpected guest, and he escapes by blowing three horns, called trumpets in the Russian text, which summon a firebird. Here the legend shows the dangerous end of her range.

So which Baba Yaga is the real one? The grandmother who tests and gives fire, or the sister who sharpens her teeth for her guest? Scholars would say the question is badly asked. Johns's three-way sorting (donor, ambiguous, evil) is a way of admitting that tellers used the same name for different jobs. Vasilisa's Baba Yaga is closest to the donor. The third sister is closest to the villain. The old woman who finds fault with a blessed girl sits in the middle.

This is where careful listeners get caught. A detail from one tale, a horn or a firebird, slips into a retelling of another, and suddenly the story makes no sense. The only defence is to know which tale a detail came from.

There is a third source of confusion: pictures. In the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, Baba Yaga appears on Russian woodblock prints called lubki. Reference works say the Russian scholar Dmitry Rovinsky, in his catalogue of Russian folk pictures of 1900, read some of them as political parody aimed at Peter the Great and Catherine I. His text has not been checked, and the idea is known only second-hand. If it is right, the old woman had already become a figure that audiences recognised so well that printmakers could dress a satire in her clothes. The prints' makers and audiences are not recorded, so this reading goes on the "told" side.

What about her relatives across the region? The atlas keeps this short and honest. West Slavic lands have Ježibaba and Baba Jaga, related names. South Slavic lands have a frightening figure, Roga, used to scare children in several countries. Ukrainian and Belarusian tellers have their own names and habits for her, and this atlas does not guess at them. We say only that the folk culture she belongs to is East Slavic, a family of Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian traditions, and that the surviving written tales we cite are Russian collections.

Why do the sisters matter? Because they change the rule. With one old woman, the tasks are a test. With three, a visit might be a trap, a loan, or a gift, depending on which sister opens the door. The first question a visitor should ask is not "what does she want?" but "which one is she?"

The three-ness also repeats across the legend. Three riders. Three pairs of hands. Three sisters. Three horns. Three days of tasks. Threes are common in folktales everywhere, and the atlas does not claim a hidden meaning behind them. They are a storyteller's rhythm, the beat that tells a listener something is about to change.

Hook: About a century and a half after the lubki, her hut walked into a concert hall. What did it look like there?

#### ch-6 · The Hut in the Concert Hall
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-6
sources: S11, S2
In the summer of 1873, the Russian artist Viktor Hartmann died suddenly, aged thirty-nine. In 1874, his friend, the composer Modest Mussorgsky, wrote a piano suite in his memory. Pictures at an Exhibition walks through a gallery of Hartmann's works. One of its movements is called "The Hut on Hen's Legs (Baba Yaga)." The picture behind it was Hartmann's design for a clock shaped like the hut on its fowl's legs. Mussorgsky added the witch's flight in her mortar. In 1922 Maurice Ravel orchestrated the suite, and many listeners first meet her through that version.

So the old woman moved from the clearing to the concert hall, and from a bone fence to a clock. This is how folklore survives: it is borrowed. In the atlas we describe modern uses in our own words and do not copy any image, film design, or game character. The goal is to learn from the long folk record, not from a modern one.

Another artist is worth naming: Ivan Bilibin, whose illustrations, including one for "Vasilisa the Beautiful" from 1900 and another from 1911, helped fix how many people picture the story. Bilibin's work is in the public domain in many places, and it is a good guide to mood: flat colour, black outlines, ornament crowding the margin. This atlas is inspired by that mood but draws new compositions of its own. We do not reproduce his plates.

Modern retellings drift. Many popular images give her a single fixed look, with a mortar always present, a hut always in the foreground, and a sinister laugh. The older tales are looser. They give her tasks, not a catchphrase. When a modern picture and an old tale disagree, this atlas follows the tale.

There is also a point about taste. Baba Yaga can be frightening without being cruel on screen. The tales say she may eat someone, and then they move on. Our retelling keeps that distance. We never show harm to a child. We show the house, the fence, the broom, the list of chores, and the quiet moment when she asks how it was done.

For readers who feel the story pulls them in: she is a folklore figure, studied by scholars, not a practice or a belief to follow. The atlas gives no instructions for contacting anything, because there is nothing to contact. She is a story people tell.

And so the legend arrives at its last page. One question is left: how much of all this can be traced to paper, and how much lives only in the telling?

Hook: Which parts of the legend can you point to in a printed source, and which ones only live in the telling?

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: baba-yaga--ch-7
sources: S1, S8, S10, S4, S5, S11
Every legend reaches a point where the storyteller has to step back and the record has to speak. This is that point.

Start with what is solid. Afanasyev's tales are in print, and the Russian text of "Baba-Yaga" (number 102) has the visitor ask the hut to turn. "Vasilisa the Beautiful" has a recorded plot: the doll, the fence, the three riders, the tasks, the question about the blessing, and the skull. "The Maiden Tsar" has three Baba Yaga sisters, a youngest one who sharpens her teeth, three trumpets and a firebird. A published review confirms that Andreas Johns, in 2004, sorted the tales into the donor, the ambiguous and the evil Baba Yaga. Hartmann died in 1873, Mussorgsky wrote his suite in 1874, and Ravel orchestrated it in 1922. These are dates and works you can check.

Some things sit between the two columns. Reference works agree that her name first appears in Lomonosov's grammar, that Afanasyev gathered nearly 600 tales, and that seven of them feature her. Those are standard facts of scholarship rather than folklore, though this atlas has not traced each one back to the page. The folklorist Andrey Toporkov's idea that the pestle came first is a scholar's suggestion, not a record.

Now what is told. That she is a "mother goddess" or a symbol of death and rebirth is an interpretation, not a record. That the fence bones belong to earlier visitors is something listeners infer, not something the tale says. That her name is linked to a word for serpent is Afanasyev's proposal, and others disagree. That the lubki are political parody is Rovinsky's reading, one scholar's interpretation of pictures.

The rule this atlas follows is simple: if a claim has no firm record behind it, it is told, not documented, however often it is repeated. Telling the two apart is the folklorist's first skill, and the most useful one to carry into the forest.

One more point on method. When two sources disagree, the atlas says so. The publication span of Afanasyev's collection is given in different ways, from 1863 to 1867. The wording of Baba Yaga's explanation of the riders differs by translation. The order of the three tasks differs between summaries. We tell the version we cite, and we say which one it is.

Notice, too, how much of the documented side is about people who wrote her down, set her to music, or argued about her, and how little is about her. A grammarian, a collector, a composer, a painter, a scholar. The record is a record of listeners. She herself never signs anything.

What survives, in the end, is not a single old woman but a habit of telling: a hut that turns, a fence that glows, a list of chores, and a question at the door. The details shift. The question stays.

Hook: The forest is quiet again. Somewhere a hut shifts its weight on its legs, and waits for the next visitor who comes asking for fire.

**v-donor**
anchor: baba-yaga--versions--v-donor
In this telling, the old woman is a hard but fair examiner. She does not simply hand over fire. She sets tasks, sleeps, comes back, and checks. She is not satisfied when she cannot find a flaw, which tells the listener that her standard is high. When she learns the helper is a mother's blessing, she says no blessed person can stay. The ending is neither a punishment nor a reward but a handing-over: fire, in a skull on a staff. This is the account that makes her a "tester," and it is the one our game's puzzles lean on when the seed picks it.

**v-devourer**
anchor: baba-yaga--versions--v-devourer
In "The Maiden Tsar," the hero Ivan meets three sisters, each a Baba Yaga. The first two do him no harm. The third is the danger: she goes to sharpen her teeth for him. He saves himself by blowing three horns and summoning a firebird. Nothing in this tale tests him with chores. Three sisters, horns or a firebird belong to this version; chores or a doll belong to another.

**v-lubok**
anchor: baba-yaga--versions--v-lubok
Late seventeenth and early eighteenth century woodblock prints show Baba Yaga in popular imagery. Dmitry Rovinsky, in a catalogue of 1900, is reported to have read some of them as parody aimed at Peter the Great and Catherine I. His text has not been checked. This is one scholar's interpretation, known second-hand, and it describes pictures, not stories. The idea that the old woman was a mask for a ruler comes from this account. It cannot explain tasks, fences or riders.

**v-scholar**
anchor: baba-yaga--versions--v-scholar
Andreas Johns sorts the Baba Yaga tales into three types: the donor, the ambiguous, and the evil. He also argues that the villainous kind may stand for a mother-in-law figure, according to a published review of his book (S10). Propp, cited separately, thought her portraits do not add up to a coherent image. This account is not a story but a method. It says: before deciding what Baba Yaga does, ask which Baba Yaga is meant.

## 7. Game world

**Setting:** The player stands in a forest clearing at dusk, outside a hut that sits on tall legs and slowly shifts its weight. The old woman is not home yet. She will return by dark, and everything must be ready.

**Scenes**

- `scene-clearing` (the fence and gate): From left to right, a leaning birch, the bone-and-skull fence with a glowing lantern-eye skull, the gate with its feet and teeth lock, a crooked well, the hut's tall legs and a ladder, a stump with a broom leaning on it, a path where three coloured stones mark where riders pass, then a dark tree line. Mood: uneasy, patient. Light: dusk glow from the horizon on the right, plus the dim skull-lamp at the fence.
- `scene-hut-inside` (the room of chores): From left to right, an iron stove with a glowing opening, a bench with an oat sack and a sieve, a cradle of hay, a window showing the forest, a table with three bowls (oats, hay, poppy), a shelf of jars and a small doll in a pocket-sized niche, a heavy carved door. Mood: warm and watchful. Light: stove fire from the left and a single candle at the table.
- `scene-night-yard` (the trial at nightfall): From left to right, the mortar at rest, a pile of straw, the fence seen from inside, three tall posts with hanging cloths (white, red, black), a pestle leaning on a post, an open cellar hatch, the gate seen from inside. Mood: tense, then quiet. Light: moon behind the hut, skull-lamps glowing along the fence.

**Spirit encounter:** The old woman arrives at dusk in her mortar, sweeping her tracks. She never attacks. She sniffs, says there is a visitor, and sets work. Each puzzle is one of her chores. A wrong answer makes her stop sweeping and look at you for a moment, and the fear meter rises with a low creak of the hut's legs. A right answer lets her grunt and move on, and the fear meter eases. At the end she asks the only question that matters: how was it done? The right reply, as the tale tells it, is the mother's blessing.

**Breaking the curse:** The final puzzle is the old woman's question. The player picks how the work was done from a shelf of cards: a mother's blessing is right in v-donor. In other seeds the answer is the matching fact for that version (for example the firebird's three horns in v-devourer). A correct answer opens the gate and earns the relic: the pocket doll of the task-passer, a small hand-carved wooden doll with a red kerchief.

**Fear events** (never block progress; static fallback when reduced motion is on)
1. The hut shifts on its legs with a long wooden creak and the lantern flickers.
2. The fence glows brighter for two seconds, then fades.
3. A distant scrape of a mortar across leaves, with a shadow crossing the window.
4. The old woman's silhouette appears in the doorway and stays still.
5. The broom sweeps the player's last tap marker away.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- Folklore, not religion or practice. No spells, rituals or "how to meet her" instructions appear anywhere. The old woman is a story figure studied by scholars.
- The threat to eat visitors is only spoken and kept mild. Never show harm to a child, never show gore. The skull is a glowing lantern prop, not a body.
- Do not copy John Wick, Hellboy, or any film, TV, game or card-game depiction of Baba Yaga. Use only the descriptions in this file.
- Ivan Bilibin's illustrations are a mood reference only. Do not copy compositions, costumes or ornaments from his plates.
- Hartmann's clock and Mussorgsky's suite are mentioned as history, with no image of any recording sleeve or score reproduced.
- Map point is the birthplace town of the collector, a public place. The legend has no single home; do not suggest travel to any forest or ruin.
- Translations of the tales differ (rider names, order of chores). Puzzles must follow the wording in this file.
- Items we could not verify from a source we read in full (tagged [R] in the fact pool; never used in true-or-told slots): the Lomonosov grammar mention of 1755 and its count; the exact span of Afanasyev's publication years (sources differ); the figure of nearly 600 tales and the count of seven Baba Yaga tales; Rovinsky's 1900 reading of the lubki; Toporkov's pestle suggestion; Ukrainian and Belarusian name forms and local variants. Johns's typology is taken from a published review (S10), not the book.
