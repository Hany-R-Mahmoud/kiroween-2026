## 1. Cold open

[Dramatization] Snow lies fresh on a sand road in the pines. A boy walks out at dawn to feed the chickens and stops. In front of him is a line of tracks, each one a small hoof, one after another. They cross the yard. They cross the fence. They go straight up the slope of the shed roof and keep going on the far side, where the snow is untouched. Nothing walked down. By noon the whole village has seen them. By night a reporter two counties away has the story, and by morning the story is bigger than the tracks. This is the tale of a creature that may never have existed, and of the people, the feuds and the newspapers that gave it a body.

## 2. Chapters

#### ch-1 · Cedar Water and Sand Roads
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-1
sources: S1, S4, S5, S7
The Pine Barrens did not get their name for being empty. Early colonists looked at the pale, sandy soil and judged it poor for farming, so they called it barren. They were wrong about what it held. Today the Pinelands National Reserve covers roughly 1.1 million acres across all or part of 56 municipalities in seven counties, created by Congress in 1978 as the first National Reserve in the country. Wetlands make up about 35 percent of it.

Walk into it and the land changes under your feet. The uplands are pitch pine, a tough tree built to survive fire. The low places hold Atlantic white cedar swamps and other wetlands. The streams are acidic and poor in nutrients, fed by a shallow aquifer under the sand, and decaying plants stain the water dark. Sand roads run between the trees, soft and pale, and they split and rejoin in ways an outsider cannot follow.

For the people who lived here, the land was a living. The iron came from the swamps. Iron-rich water from streams and bogs left ore along the banks, and colonists mined and smelted it from the 1760s to the 1860s. The fuel was charcoal, made by colliers who burned native pine in smoky mounds of sand and turf. Furnaces at places such as Martha, Weymouth and Atsion supplied iron for ammunition and armaments. Whole towns grew up around a furnace, and the most famous, Batsto, may have held about 500 people at its height. Then the discovery of coal beside the ore beds of Pennsylvania in the 1840s doomed the local industry, and the last great furnace went cold in the 1860s.

This is the background you need for what comes next. The Barrens were large, quiet and poorly mapped by outsiders. A town of ironworkers could sit hours by cart from the nearest newspaper. The people who lived in the pines were sometimes called "pineys," a word that outsiders used with contempt and that locals have often claimed back. Stories grew easily in that distance. A rumor could cross the sand roads for fifty years and never meet anyone who could check it.

Several local legends circulate in the region: a ghost of a famous pirate captain, a black dog on the roads, a white stag, a mysterious doctor. They are told as campfire material, and no records back them as fact. Among them, one name rises above the rest, and it does not start with a monster. It starts with a family name painted on the front of a printed book.

The first oddity is the name. In the earliest printed references, the creature is not called the Jersey Devil at all. Printed references from the 1800s call it the "Leeds Devil," or the "Devil of Leeds." A Leeds. A family, with a surname, in a particular place at the edge of the pines, named Leeds Point. Why would a monster carry a family's name, and what had that family done to deserve it?

To answer that, the story has to leave the swamp and enter a print shop in colonial Pennsylvania, where two men were fighting over who could predict the future better.

Hook: A monster named after a family, in a place that seems made for secrets. To learn why, follow the name to a colonial print shop and a very public quarrel.

#### ch-2 · The Leeds Quarrel
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-2
sources: S3, S9, S10, S11
Daniel Leeds lived from 1651 to 1720. He was a Quaker in South Jersey and a printer of almanacs. An almanac in that era was the most-read book in a house after the Bible: tides, moon phases, planting dates, weather guesses, and often astrology. That last ingredient got Leeds in trouble. Many Quakers of the time considered astrology improper, and Leeds' fellow Friends objected to his almanacs. He left the community.

Leeds did not go without a fight. He later became an Anglican and aligned himself with the colony's unpopular royal governor. He published material against the Quakers. In a small colony where politics, religion and printing were tangled together, that made him an enemy to many powerful neighbors. Accusations that he served the devil were a standard way to attack a man who crossed the wrong side. The historian Brian Regal of Kean University argues that this is where the "Leeds Devil" label began, as political mud thrown at a family, not as a report of an animal.

After Daniel's death, his son Titan continued the almanac business. Titan did something bold. From 1728 he printed the family crest on his almanacs, and the crest showed a wyvern, a winged dragon-like creature. Readers who saw that image on the cover of the Leeds almanac each year were looking at a winged beast attached to the family name. Historians who favor this theory point to the crest as a visual seed, a monster that was already on the family's stationery before anyone told a story about a birth.

Then came Benjamin Franklin. He was young, he was starting his own almanac, and he needed the market that the Leeds almanac held. In 1733 Franklin published a prediction, with a straight face and a joking heart, that Titan Leeds would die on October 17 of that year, to the minute. Titan denied it in print the following year. Franklin kept the joke going, and in his 1735 almanac he treated Titan as a ghost. A man whose family was already wrapped in rumors about astrology and the devil was now being publicly called a dead man walking.

This feud proves some things and not others. It documents that the Leeds family was lampooned, that their almanacs carried a winged crest and that their name was attached to talk of the devil. It does not document a creature. It does not document a thirteenth child. It builds a trail of printed insults and a family brand, which is exactly the kind of material from which a legend can be assembled later, by people who forgot the original joke.

Regal and his co-author wrote a book on this theory, titled "The Secret History of the Jersey Devil." It is one reading. Others prefer the tale told by the families of the pines, which has nothing to do with Franklin at all, and a great deal to do with a mother and a night of pain.

Hook: Politics and almanacs gave the Leeds name a devil's reputation. But the pines tell a different origin, one with a mother, a birth and thirteen children.

#### ch-3 · The Thirteenth Child
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-3
sources: S3, S4, S9
In the tale told around the Pine Barrens, the story begins with a woman called Mother Leeds. She had borne twelve children, the legend says, and she was expecting a thirteenth. In most tellings the year is 1735. Exhausted, afraid, or simply angry, she cried out that this child might as well be a devil. The exact words change with the teller. One version has her say the child should be the devil. Another has her say this one should be the devil. The meaning is the same, and so is the dread.

[Dramatization] Imagine a one-room house on a sand road, shutters closed, a fire of pitch pine popping and spitting resin. A midwife waits. A wind comes up out of the cedar swamp and bends the flames flat. The child is born, and for a moment is only a child. Then, as the tale tells it, it changes. It grows. It stretches. It leaves through the chimney, and the pines take it.

What does the creature look like? Tellers disagree. One popular description gives it a tail, hooves and wings, and an appetite for animals. Others give it the head of a horse, or a dog, or a goat; others give it bat wings; others say it screams. No two of these descriptions agree. A legend that was reported by one witness would have one description. A legend that was built from many mouths over many winters has dozens.

Even the mother is a puzzle. Some storytellers place her at Estellville, a place in the pines near Mays Landing. Others place her at Leeds Point, a place that carries the family name. Some tellings call her Mary. Some writers call her Deborah, because a Japhet Leeds, husband of a Deborah, is reported to have named twelve children in a will written in 1736. That fits "twelve and then one" neatly, and the match has been repeated for generations. But the link is a guess made long afterward. A will naming twelve children says nothing about a thirteenth, a curse or a creature, and historians have not identified a real Mother Leeds in the records. The legend's 1735 date also comes before that will, not after.

The legend also collected visitors. One tale says that Commodore Stephen Decatur, the famous naval officer, was at a works in Hanover when the creature crossed the sky, and he fired a cannonball clean through it, which changed nothing. Another says that Joseph Bonaparte, the brother of Napoleon, who lived on an estate at Bordentown, saw the creature while out hunting around 1820. Both are told with relish. Historians who looked through the papers of both men found no sign of either encounter. Famous names are a common way for a rumor to make itself sound grown-up.

In 1859 the Atlantic Monthly ran what the historian Brian Regal calls the earliest published mention of the "Leeds Devil." Its writer looked down on the people of the pines, yet repeated their tales of a monster that harmed children. The thirteenth-child story had by then become a way of explaining things that went wrong in the pines: a lost hen, a dead calf, a noise at night.

Still, for well over a century, the creature belonged to the pines. It was a scary bedtime story for children and a hard-luck excuse for farmers. Then, in the winter of 1909, it walked out of the swamp and into the front page of every newspaper in the Delaware Valley.

Hook: A mother's angry words and twelve earlier children made a perfect story. But no one would have heard it outside the pines, without one cold week in January.

#### ch-4 · Seven Days in January
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-4
sources: S1, S2, S4, S8
The panic of 1909 is usually dated from January 16 to January 23. It begins with footprints, which is fitting. On or around January 16, reports surfaced in South Jersey of odd tracks in the snow. They were said to look like hoofprints, and they were said to cross places no animal should be able to cross: fences, yards, even rooftops.

By January 17 the story had a human witness. According to the Philadelphia Inquirer's account, a Gloucester City man named Nelson Evans said that he and his wife had seen the creature around 2:30 in the morning. Their account, as reported, was the first of many. Newspapers in the region began to publish what readers sent in and what reporters heard. Within days there were hundreds of claimed encounters.

The accounts spread fast. Reports came from Haddon Heights, where the creature was said to have attacked a trolley car, and from Camden, where it was said to have attacked a social club. Newspapers described police in Camden and in Bristol, Pennsylvania firing at it without effect. Sightings were reported in Collingswood. They were reported across the Delaware River in Philadelphia and as far away as Delaware and Maryland. The Evening Bulletin of Philadelphia is said to have published an illustration, giving readers a shape to fear.

The reports also described the response. Schools and factories were said to have closed for a time. Armed groups were said to have roamed the pines. Here we must be careful. A modern Inquirer history feature repeats the general picture of closed schools and factories. But exact school names, mill names and dates do not appear in the checked sources. Some modern websites name whole towns as shut down for the week, and those claims trace to later retellings, not to any checked period newspaper.

A reward is another example. Many retellings say the Philadelphia Zoo offered a reward of ten thousand dollars for the capture of the creature. The Inquirer's own history feature says its coverage from the time does not mention it, and a zoo spokesperson found no record of it in the 1909 board minutes. Whether anyone ever offered such a reward is unclear. The number is repeated because it sounds large, and it sounds large because newspapers love a large number.

On January 23 the story took a sudden turn: the creature was reported captured, in Fairmount Park in Philadelphia. Captured, said the papers. And yet no cage ever opened to a scientist. No specimen went to a university. The tracks stopped. The week ended, as panics do, not with an answer but with fatigue.

Look at the pattern from a distance. Hoofprints in snow have simple explanations. The people who write about the footprints of that week note that they looked like the hooves of horses, and that ordinary animals or a prankster could have left them. A winter's night, a nervous town, a morning newspaper, and a rumor with a famous name. That is a recipe for mass worry, and historians who study these events have described the 1909 wave as exactly that.

But if the tracks were faked and the sightings were rumor, someone still had to light the match. And in Philadelphia, a rumor said that someone had a very good reason to.

Hook: A week of tracks, trolleys and closed doors. But who had the most to gain from keeping the panic alive?

#### ch-5 · The Dime Museum Cage
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-5
sources: S1, S2
At Ninth and Arch Streets in Philadelphia stood a dime museum, a kind of cheap, crowded show house of the era, where a ticket paid for curiosities, wax figures and living oddities. The press agent for the museum was a Philadelphia publicity man named Norman Jeffries. Sometimes his name is spelled Jefferies, and the spelling matters when you search old records.

According to the Inquirer, the museum began to advertise that it had the Jersey Devil, alive and on display, in January 1909. The creature was billed under names such as the "Leeds Devil." According to later reports, it was a kangaroo, dressed up. The details are almost comic. Paraffin and copper sheet were said to have formed the wings. Horsehair was said to have been added for whiskers. Green stripes were said to have been painted along the body. The creature was chained in a steel cage, and a public that had read about the terror in the pines now paid to see it up close. Thousands came, according to the report, over several weeks. By 1911 the exhibit was gone.

The sources disagree at this point, and the disagreement is the point. One popular account says that Jeffries and a partner staged the hoax, and that Jeffries himself admitted it about twenty years afterwards. That account rests on books and websites, and no dated newspaper confession has been traced. The Inquirer's own history feature says only that in 1929 the paper reported that Jeffries had largely engineered the panic to bring in visitors. It does not describe a confession. When Jeffries died in 1933, his obituary in the Inquirer said he had "used all the arts of a press agent" to build belief in the legend. It does not say he made the footprints. It does not say he invented the creature.

So the hoax claim has layers. The museum exhibit looks solidly reported: there was an advertisement, and there was a creature in a cage. The claim that the same press agent started the entire January panic, that he made the tracks, that he sent the false reports, is a bigger claim, and the available record only partly supports it. The 1929 report and the 1933 obituary show that the paper believed Jeffries had profited from belief in the legend.

The hoax leaves some things unexplained. The Leeds name was printed in almanacs and magazines long before 1909. The tales of hoofprints and livestock losses are older than any dime museum. Whoever turned January 1909 into a profit used a legend that was already lying in wait, like a match placed in a drawer for a century.

There is one more reason this chapter matters to the investigation. A hoax needs witnesses, and witnesses need to tell the story their own way. Some who saw the cage believed it was the true creature. Some who read about the footprints believed they were made by the cage's occupant. Some insisted the cage held nothing from the pines at all. Three tellers, three versions, and a ledger of dates that does not line up between them.

In the months afterward the pines went quiet. But the name stayed in the mouths of children, and in the margins of newspapers, and then it kept going, long after the cage was gone.

Hook: A cage, a press agent and a painted kangaroo. If the monster was sold for a dime, why does it still walk out of the pines?

#### ch-6 · A State Demon Without a Law
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-6
sources: S3, S6, S12
After 1909 the Devil never really went away. It simply changed hands, from farm families to newspapers, from newspapers to roadside stories, and finally to everyone.

The reports continue through the twentieth century. In December 1925 a farmer in Greenwich Township shot an unidentified animal that had been raiding his chickens. In July 1937 residents of Downingtown, Pennsylvania reported an animal with red eyes. In 1951 boys in Gibbstown, New Jersey said they saw a monster. In 1960 tracks and noises were reported near Mays Landing, and merchants in Camden are said to have offered a reward of ten thousand dollars for any captured Devil. Popular retellings give each of these as a newspaper report, and we have not opened the originals. In none of these cases did anyone produce a creature.

People have tried to explain the sightings. Some suggest that a lost sandhill crane, a tall gray bird that can look eerie at dusk, may have inspired a few reports. Others point to ordinary animals, hoaxes, and the human talent for seeing patterns in the dark. Historians of mass panic point out that once a story is in the newspapers, every odd noise in the woods starts to count as a sighting. The 1909 wave, they say, is a textbook example.

The legend has also entered the culture in a hundred ways. In 1982, a statewide contest let fans vote on a name for a new professional hockey team, and "Devils" won, after the creature. That team's emblem belongs to the team; the creature in these pages is drawn only from the old descriptions. There are also local festivals, souvenir shops, and a state tradition of laughing about the Devil in the same breath as fearing it.

One modern myth deserves a correction. Many people say the Jersey Devil is the official state demon of New Jersey, by law. The New Jersey State Library searched the 1939 session laws and found no law naming the Devil. It traces the idea to the Federal Writers' Project guide of that year, which gave the Leeds Devil the title of "official State demon" by default, not by statute. Because the claim sounds so official, it spreads easily, and that is a small lesson in how legends travel: someone says it with confidence, someone repeats it, and soon it looks like a record.

Consider what these later sightings have in common. They are brief. They are told by a witness at the edge of a road or a field. They are reported in newspapers before they are examined. And they cluster around places the legend already named: the Pine Barrens, the sand roads, the swamps. A monster that has been described for generations is easy to see, because people already know what they are looking for.

[Dramatization] Tonight, far from any town, a driver on a long straight road in the pines thinks he sees a shape cross the headlights. It is gone before he can brake. He tells his wife. His wife tells a friend. The next evening, the friend tells a stranger at a diner. And that is how, once again, a story begins.

Which leaves the question the whole investigation has been circling. When we strip away the hoax, the feud, the tracks, the farmers and the cage, what is actually left?

Hook: The creature has appeared in every decade since, always in a witness's story and never in a laboratory. Time to separate what the record holds from what the pines whispered.

#### ch-7 · What Is Documented, and What Is Told
anchor: jersey-devil--ch-7
sources: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S9, S10
Let us put the evidence on the table, plainly, the way a careful investigator would.

First, the setting. The Pine Barrens are real, and so are the iron furnaces, the cedar swamps and the sand roads. The Pinelands National Reserve is documented by the state's own fact sheets. That gives the legend a believable stage.

Second, the family. Daniel Leeds, Titan Leeds and their almanacs are documented. So are the Quaker dispute, the political quarrel and Benjamin Franklin's joke prediction of Titan's death. The winged crest on the almanacs from 1728 is documented. This is the strongest historical foundation for the name "Leeds Devil," and it is why careful historians favor the almanac theory over the birth story.

Third, the news. The week of January 16 to 23, 1909 is documented in the newspapers of the time. People reported tracks. People reported attacks on a trolley and a social club. People reported a capture. Those are reports, and the fact that they were printed is documented. That the events they describe actually happened is not.

Fourth, the exhibit. The museum at Ninth and Arch advertised a Jersey Devil in January 1909. The press agent Norman Jeffries and his obituary are documented. The more dramatic claim, that he alone created the whole panic, and the further claim that he confessed, are not firmly documented.

Now the other side of the table. The birth in 1735 is told, not recorded. The name of the mother changes with the teller. The twelve children match a 1736 will that writers cite, but the will says nothing about a curse. The mother is Mary in the tellings and Deborah in later guesses. The words of her cry change from one telling to the next. Decatur's cannonball and Bonaparte's hunting trip are told, and historians found nothing in their papers. The ten thousand dollar reward from the zoo is told, and the zoo found no record of it. A general report of closed schools and factories appears in a modern retrospective, but no checked source names the towns or mills.

And the monster itself, the wings, the hooves, the scream, the horse head, belongs entirely to the telling. No body, no bones, no photograph that has survived scrutiny.

The lesson of this legend is simple. A legend is rarely one thing. It is a name, a quarrel, a rumor, a profit and a fear, stacked on each other over time. When two witnesses tell you different versions, one of them may be wrong, and both of them may be wrong, and the only way to know is to ask what each version needs to be true. Check the dates. Check who gains. Check whether the detail appears in more than one place, or only in the mouth of one confident teller.

The pines are still there, and so are the people who will swear to one version or another. Listen to all of them. Then trust the paper trail over the story.

Hook: The monster is a stack of names and newspapers. Now find out which layer is true.

##### v-mother-leeds
anchor: jersey-devil--versions--v-mother-leeds
This is the version told in the pines. A woman named Leeds, already mother to twelve, is expecting a thirteenth child in 1735. In a moment of anger or exhaustion she says the child might as well be a devil. The child is born, changes and flies away. Storytellers place her at Estellville, near Mays Landing. Other tellings put her at Leeds Point. The creature is said to have wings, hooves and a tail. This version never mentions almanacs. Its power is its simplicity, and it travels well because it has a villain, a victim and a warning. Where it breaks down is evidence: no birth record, no named child and no single wording of the mother's words. If this version is true for your run, look for clues that mention a mother, a thirteenth child and a house on a sand road.

##### v-almanac-feud
anchor: jersey-devil--versions--v-almanac-feud
This version belongs to the historians. Daniel Leeds was a Quaker printer whose almanacs carried astrology, and he left his community over it. He became an Anglican and took the side of the royal governor. His son Titan continued the almanac business and printed a winged crest on it from 1728. In 1733 Benjamin Franklin joked that Titan would die that October, and in 1735 he teased Titan as a ghost. The "devil" in "Leeds Devil," this version says, was political slander that stuck to the name. There is no creature and no thirteenth child. Clues for this version involve almanacs, crests, printers and dates.

##### v-1909-witnesses
anchor: jersey-devil--versions--v-1909-witnesses
In this version a real animal, or something no one can name, moved through South Jersey and Philadelphia for a week in January 1909. Tracks appeared in snow, a man said he saw it at 2:30 in the morning on January 17, and a trolley in Haddon Heights and a social club in Camden were said to be attacked. Police in Camden and Bristol are said to have fired on it. On January 23 it was said to be captured in Fairmount Park. The version relies on the accounts of newspapers. It does not explain why no specimen was ever examined. Clues for this version involve hoofprints, newspaper clippings and trolley timetables.

##### v-dime-museum
anchor: jersey-devil--versions--v-dime-museum
The skeptic's version says the panic was a publicity opportunity. A Philadelphia press agent named Norman Jeffries, working for the dime museum at Ninth and Arch, is said to have exhibited a kangaroo made to look like the Devil, with paraffin and copper sheet wings, horsehair whiskers and green stripes. The creature drew crowds and was gone by 1911. Whether Jeffries confessed, and when, differs between tellers, and the Inquirer's history feature reports no confession. The safest wording is the Inquirer's: in 1929 it reported that he largely engineered the panic, and in his 1933 obituary it said he used the arts of a press agent to build belief. Clues for this version involve show posters, cages and ticket stubs.

## 6. Leads and dead ends

```
lead-id: lead-1
starts from: v-mother-leeds
steps: 1) Inspect the cradle with a tiny hoof carved on its side in the sand-road cabin. 2) Read the family bible page and count the names. 3) Recall the name of the community the story places her in and search the road map for it.
dead end: The bible page lists twelve names and nothing more; no thirteenth name, no curse, only a will-like list. The map shows two candidate places, so the story cannot fix one.
contradiction: f-16 and jersey-devil--ch-3 (the place changes with the teller) and jersey-devil--ch-7 (a list of twelve is not a record of a curse)
can be true when: the seed picks v-mother-leeds as the true version
```

```
lead-id: lead-2
starts from: v-almanac-feud
steps: 1) Inspect the printing press in the furnace-office loft and find the almanac cover. 2) Match the winged crest to its year. 3) Read the October 1733 prediction page and decide who wrote it.
dead end: The press holds only almanacs, crests and a joke prediction; no page mentions a child, a curse or a creature.
contradiction: f-13 and jersey-devil--ch-2 (the feud gives the name, not a birth), and f-14 with jersey-devil--ch-3 (a birth story exists only in the telling)
can be true when: the seed picks v-almanac-feud as the true version
```

```
lead-id: lead-3
starts from: v-1909-witnesses
steps: 1) Examine the tracks in the snow outside the sand-road cabin and see where they stop. 2) Read the January 17 clipping and note the hour. 3) Find the trolley schedule and check which town is named.
dead end: The tracks stop at a rooftop with a fresh footprint of a boot-heel pressed into the same snow; the clipping repeats the hour but names no second witness.
contradiction: f-23 and jersey-devil--ch-4 (tracks looked like horse hooves and could have been left by ordinary animals or a prankster), and jersey-devil--ch-5 (a paid exhibit used the same fear)

```

```
lead-id: lead-4
starts from: v-dime-museum
steps: 1) Inspect the show poster in the newsroom for the museum's street corner. 2) Read the cage invoice for the animal named. 3) Check the 1929 clipping against the 1933 obituary and compare their wording.
dead end: The 1929 clipping says the press agent engineered the panic; the obituary says only that he used a press agent's arts. Neither says he confessed.
```

## 7. Game world

### Setting
The player stands on a pale sand road at dusk in the New Jersey pines, beside a cabin, a furnace village and, later, a small town newspaper office. The year feels like 1909, with traces of the colonial past in old almanacs and tools.

### Scenes

**scene-1: The Sand Road Cabin.** Left to right: a leaning cedar-shingle cabin with a dark chimney, a cradle on the porch, a chicken coop with a fence, the long sand road crossing the frame, a cedar swamp with tea-colored water and reeds, and a leaning signpost with no letters. Mood: cold and watchful, a thin snow on the ground. Light sources: a single oil lantern on the porch; moon glow on the water.

**scene-2: The Furnace Loft.** Left to right: a brick stack and cooled furnace mouth, a heap of bog-iron pigs, a wooden press with an almanac cover, a long table with ledgers and spectacles, a stack of cast iron stove plates, a window onto the pine road. Mood: dusty, close and secretive. Light sources: a ceiling lamp, orange glow from the old furnace mouth.

**scene-3: The Newsroom, January 1909.** Left to right: a wall of pinned clippings, a type case, a desk with a lamp and a cup, a rolled show poster, a cage chain on a hook, a cabinet of plaster hooves, a frosted window with trolley sparks outside. Mood: urgent and uneasy. Light sources: a green-shaded desk lamp and trolley sparks outside.

### Spirit encounter
The figure appears as a tall shadow among cedar trunks, shaped like a long-necked animal with wing edges, barely lit by lantern light, never in full view at first. It asks one question at a time, in the form of a riddle about a name, a date or a place. A wrong answer makes it step closer and raises the fear meter; the lantern dims, and the sound of wind grows. A right answer makes it step back and the lantern steadies. The figure never touches the player. It pauses, as if listening for a different story to be told.

### Breaking the curse
The final puzzle asks the player to sort a set of statements into "Documented" and "Told" using the atlas, then to name which version their evidence supports. A correct sort and a correct version earns the relic: a small iron lantern with a wyvern-crest pane, which stays lit when the figure is near. The curse is the story itself. When the player names where the claim comes from, the figure fades.

### Fear events
1. Wind rises through the pines and a distant scream plays once; the screen vignette tightens (muted under reduced motion).
2. The porch lantern flickers and returns to steady; no movement beyond the flicker.
3. A winged shadow crosses the moon glow on the water, a slow pass of a minute; under reduced motion it is replaced by a still image.
4. Hoof taps sound from the rooftop of the cabin; a short audio cue with a text caption.
5. A clipped newspaper page flutters from the wall in scene-3; a still alternative is offered. None of these block progress.

## 11. Sensitivity and IP notes

- The creature's name includes "devil." This atlas treats it only as folklore and a media panic. There is no religious framing, no scripture and no occult instruction anywhere in the text or puzzles.
- A professional hockey team uses this creature's name. Do not use or describe its logo, colors-as-brand or mascot. The spirit design here is our own: a long-necked, winged shadow with hooves, not the pitchfork-and-horns trope.
- Do not copy film or television depictions of the creature. Use the prompts as written.
- No quotes are invented for real people. The only words attributed to the legend's mother are the folklore phrases, labelled as told. Stephen Decatur, Joseph Bonaparte, Benjamin Franklin, Norman Jeffries and Nelson Evans are described only in paraphrase.
- Scenes marked `[Dramatization]` are imagined.
- The Pine Barrens are a real, partly protected landscape. Nothing here encourages visiting closed furnace ruins, private land or hazardous swamps.
- "Pineys" can be a slur depending on speaker. The text mentions it once with context. Translators should handle it carefully.
- The family names Leeds and Estell are real historical family names. Keep the tone aimed at the legend and the colonial feud, not at descendants, and give no private home addresses.
- Claims that could not be verified from a source are marked as "told" in section 4: specific school and mill closures in named towns, the zoo reward, the Decatur and Bonaparte encounters, the claim that Jeffries confessed, and the identity of Mother Leeds (Mary or Deborah).
